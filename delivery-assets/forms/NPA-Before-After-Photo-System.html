Section 01
Why Consistency Is Everything
The business case for taking clinical photos the same way every single time
Inconsistent before/after photos don't just look unprofessional — they actively undermine your results. A patient who looks slightly to the left in their "before" and straight ahead in their "after" appears to have worse results than they actually do, even if the treatment was perfect. The photo system is not about photography. It's about showing your work fairly and accurately.

What inconsistency costs you

  • Results look worse than they are — loses potential clients
  • Client unhappy because the "before" photo flatters them more than the "after"
  • No legal protection in a dispute — unusable for documentation
  • Cannot be used for marketing — embarrassing to post
  • Cannot track treatment progress accurately over time

What consistency gives you

  • Marketing content that actually converts followers to clients
  • Legal documentation that protects you in a complaint
  • Pre-existing asymmetry documented and acknowledged
  • Compelling before/after series that tells a treatment story
  • Professional presentation that builds instant trust
Section 02
Your Photo Setup
Lighting, background, distance, and positioning — the non-negotiables
Lighting — The Most Important Variable
Lighting is responsible for 80% of photo quality variation. Different lighting between before and after makes results look worse, not better. Standardize your lighting completely — meaning the same setup, same time of day (or controlled artificial light), same position relative to the patient, every single time.

Use a ring light — not window lightNon-negotiable

Window light changes by time of day, weather, and season. A ring light gives you identical, reproducible lighting every single time. Position it at eye level, directly in front of the patient. Use the same ring light, same position, every photo.

Daylight-balanced bulb temperature

5500–6500K color temperature. This is "daylight" balanced — skin tones appear natural and consistent. Avoid warm bulbs (3000K) — they make skin look more golden in one photo vs. another.

Close blinds and control ambient light

If you have windows in your treatment room, close the blinds before every photo session. Ambient light changes everything — a sunny morning before vs. a cloudy afternoon after creates false visual inconsistency.

Background

Solid, neutral background — alwaysRequired

White, light gray, or nude wall. No cabinets, shelves, products, or equipment visible. A branded photo backdrop ($20–40 on Amazon) mounted in a consistent location is ideal. Use the exact same spot for every facial photo.

Patient positioning

Patient seated or standing, shoulders relaxed, face relaxed and neutral (not smiling — smile changes face shape dramatically). Hair off face and pulled back the same way in before and after. No makeup for facial photos — or consistent makeup in both before and after if agreed.

Distance and Height

Standardized distance protocol

  • Face: Camera approximately 18–24 inches from the face. Patient fills the frame from chin to hairline with 1–2 inches of space on each side.
  • Body areas (legs, arms, abdomen): Step back to capture the full treatment area plus 6–8 inches on all sides for context.
  • Camera height: Eye level for facial photos. Mark a spot on the wall or use a tripod with a fixed height so it's identical every time.
  • Pro tip: Put tape on the floor where the patient stands and a mark on the wall where the camera goes. Takes the guesswork out completely.
Section 03 — Required Angles by Treatment Area

Botox / Neurotoxins

Anterior (straight on) · Left and right lateral (full side profile) · Left and right oblique (3/4 view) · At rest AND during expression (raise brows, frown, squint, smile) · Forehead lines: capture full forehead in frame

Dermal Fillers

Anterior · Left and right lateral · Left and right oblique · Lips: straight on + slight chin-down angle to show upper lip · Cheeks: oblique most important · Jawline: lateral and oblique from slightly below

Laser / IPL / Resurfacing

Anterior · Both obliques · Document specific lesion locations before treatment · Consistent lighting critical — shadows mask pigmentation changes · No foundation or BB cream in before photos

Body Treatments (Morpheus8, Lipo, Weight Loss)

Anterior · Left and right lateral · Posterior (back) · Same clothing or gown in before and after · Same posture — neutral stance, arms relaxed at sides · Same time of day if possible (bloating varies)

Brows / Lashes / Waxing

Anterior · Close-up crop of brow/lash area from straight on · Eyes open neutral, eyes lightly closed · No makeup in before for brow photos · Consistent zoom/crop ratio in before and after

Microneedling / RF

Anterior · Both obliques · Close-up of specific concern area (texture, scarring, pores) · Series photos at consistent intervals (Day 0, Day 7, Month 1, Month 3, Month 6) · Document healing stages

Section 04
iPhone Camera Settings
The exact settings for consistent, professional clinical photos with no filters, no editing
You do not need a DSLR or a professional camera. Your iPhone takes clinically adequate photos if you use the right settings consistently. The goal is accurate, unmodified documentation — not artistic photography.

iPhone Settings — Clinical Photo Mode

Camera modePhoto (not Portrait mode — distorts features)
FlashOFF — always. Use ring light only.
HDROFF — creates inconsistent processing
Live PhotoOFF — only need still image
Zoom1x only — no digital zoom, no telephoto
FiltersNONE — never apply any filter to clinical photos
Timer3-second timer or use a tripod — eliminates camera shake
GridON — helps with consistent framing and alignment
ResolutionHighest available — Settings → Camera → Formats → High Efficiency or Most Compatible

Never edit clinical photos

Do not adjust brightness, contrast, saturation, or any other parameter on clinical before/after photos. Do not use Lightroom, VSCO, Facetune, or any editing app. A modified clinical photo is not only misleading — it exposes you to fraud and FTC enforcement actions. What the photo captures is what your documentation must show.

The Tripod Solution — $25 That Changes Everything

Recommended setup

A basic flexible tripod (Joby GorillaPod or similar, $25–40) mounted at eye level. Mark the floor with tape where the tripod goes. Mark the tripod height with tape. This eliminates the single biggest source of inconsistency — handheld camera wobble and varying heights. Set it once, use it forever.

Section 05 — The Photo Workflow

Before every treatment — no exceptionsRequired

Confirm photo consent is signed. Position patient in photo spot. Set up ring light. Take all required angles. Review on screen to confirm focus and framing before patient leaves the photo spot. Transfer to HIPAA-compliant storage before end of day.

Naming convention — use this system

LastnameFirstname_YYYYMMDD_Area_Angle. Example: AlcalaDanielle_20260115_Forehead_Anterior.jpg. This makes photos findable, sortable, and datable without opening each file. Use the same system for every patient every time.

After photo timing

Botox: at 14-day follow-up (peak results). Filler: at 14-day follow-up (swelling resolved). Laser/IPL: at 4-6 week follow-up AND at 3 months. Microneedling: at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months. Weight loss: monthly at weigh-in.

Section 06 — HIPAA-Compliant Storage

Your iPhone camera roll is NOT HIPAA compliant

Patient photos stored in a personal camera roll are not HIPAA compliant. If your phone is lost, stolen, or backed up to a personal iCloud account that is shared or breached — that is a HIPAA violation. Transfer photos to a HIPAA-compliant platform immediately after each session.

HIPAA-compliant photo storage options

  • Your EHR/EMR system — most have built-in photo upload with Business Associate Agreement (BAA). This is ideal.
  • Google Workspace (with BAA) — Google will sign a BAA for healthcare providers. Google Drive with BAA = compliant.
  • Microsoft 365 (with BAA) — similar to Google. OneDrive with healthcare BAA.
  • Dropbox Business (with BAA) — Business plan includes BAA availability.

Transfer workflow

  • Take photos on iPhone
  • Immediately transfer to HIPAA-compliant storage (same day)
  • Rename using naming convention
  • Delete from iPhone camera roll after confirmed upload
  • Never text patient photos from your personal phone — use HIPAA-compliant messaging

Posting Guidelines & Consent Script
What you can post, what you can't, and how to get permission the right way
Section 07 — What You Can and Cannot Post

Written marketing consent required before posting anythingNon-negotiable

Clinical consent (for the medical record) does NOT authorize marketing use. You need a separate, specific consent for marketing/social media use. This is your Photo & HIPAA Authorization form — included in the NPA Consent Form Bundle.

FTC endorsement guidelines — required disclosures

The FTC requires disclosure when before/after photos represent typical or atypical results. If results shown are not typical for your average patient, you must disclose: "Results may vary. Individual results are not guaranteed." Post this caption on every before/after you share.

What you can post with full consent

Before/after photos where the patient has signed full marketing consent. Identifiable facial photos, body photos, name if patient agrees. Patient testimonials with their written consent. Tagged social media posts the patient initiates.

What you can post with anonymized consent only

Photos cropped to remove identifying features. Body area photos that don't include face. Photos where the face is blurred or cropped. Never combine with the patient's name, location, or any detail that could identify them.

What you can never post regardless of consent

Photos of minors in any form. Photos that are misleading or digitally altered to enhance results. Any clinical detail in the caption that constitutes PHI. Photos of patients who are intoxicated, incapacitated, or could not meaningfully consent.

Section 08 — The Photo Consent Conversation Script
How Danielle asks for photo consent at Hello Gorgeous
"I ask for photo consent at the beginning of every new patient's first appointment — before we do anything. I keep it casual and warm, not like I'm reading them their rights. Here's exactly how I do it."

The ask — word for word

"Before we get started, I take clinical photos at every appointment — it's how I track your progress over time and make sure I'm always giving you the best results. Those photos stay private in your chart. I have a consent form that covers that — it's part of what you already signed.

Now — completely separately and totally optional — I also love to share before/afters when I get a result I'm really proud of. If we get a great result today, would you be open to me sharing your photos on Instagram? [Pause.] No pressure at all if not — I'll take just as good care of you either way. If you say yes, I'll send you the post before I share anything."

The magic sentence is "I'll send you the post before I share anything." Clients who would have said no say yes because they know they'll have approval. And it means you never post anything surprising — which protects your relationship with the patient long-term.
