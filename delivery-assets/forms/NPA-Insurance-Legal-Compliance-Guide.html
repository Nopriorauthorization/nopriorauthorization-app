Section 01
The Year-by-Year Journey
What compliance looked like at Hello Gorgeous from Day One to Year Ten — the honest version
Nobody handed me a compliance checklist when I opened Hello Gorgeous. I found things out when I needed them — sometimes the hard way. This section is what I know now, organized by when it typically becomes relevant for a growing med spa.
From Danielle
"When I opened Hello Gorgeous, I was a licensed esthetician with a dream, a name from my aunt who raised me, and absolutely no idea what a collaborative practice agreement was. I figured out compliance the way most of us do — by asking questions, making mistakes, and being terrified of doing something wrong. This guide is everything I wish I'd had on Day One."
Before You Open

The foundation — business structure, licensing, and basic insurance

Form your LLC or PLLC before you open the door. Get your general liability and professional liability insurance in place. Confirm your state licensing requirements — esthetician license, facility license, and any business permits required by your city or county. This is non-negotiable. You need these before your first client walks in.

Year 1

Adding medical services — the rules change completely

The moment you add Botox, fillers, or any prescription service, you are operating as a medical practice in most states. This is where a collaborative practice agreement (CPA) or medical director relationship becomes critical. The rules for who can perform what and who must supervise vary dramatically by state. This is the year most people get this wrong — including me.

Year 2–3

HIPAA, OSHA, and the paperwork you didn't know existed

HIPAA compliance isn't optional the moment you collect patient health information — which is Day One. OSHA requirements for bloodborne pathogen training, sharps disposal, and exposure control plans kick in the first time you use a needle. Most small practices don't have these in place until they're forced to. Get ahead of it.

Year 3–5

DEA registration, controlled substances, and prescription management

Adding testosterone, GLP-1 medications, hormone therapy, or other controlled or prescription substances requires DEA registration for your prescribing provider and a whole new compliance layer around storage, dispensing logs, and inventory. This is not complicated once you know the rules — but you have to know the rules.

Year 5–7

Employees, HR compliance, and expanding your liability footprint

The moment you hire your first employee, you take on employment law obligations — workers' comp insurance, payroll taxes, employee handbooks, and documentation requirements. You also need to make sure your malpractice coverage extends to your staff's actions. Most providers underestimate how much their liability exposure grows with each hire.

Year 7–10

Annual compliance reviews — because the rules keep changing

At 10 years in, I still review my insurance coverage annually, verify my collaborative practice agreement is current, and check for regulatory changes in Illinois that affect my services. Compliance isn't a one-time task. It's an ongoing practice. The providers who get in trouble are the ones who set it up once and never revisit it.

The honest truth
"I did not have everything perfectly in place from Day One. I don't know a single med spa owner who did. What matters is that you build the right foundation as early as possible and that you know what you don't know so you can go find the right person to fill the gap. That's what this guide is for."
Section 02
The Insurance Stack
The policies every med spa and esthetic practice needs — what they are, what they cover, and what to ask for
Insurance is not an area to cut corners. One lawsuit, one patient complaint, one employee injury without the right coverage — and you're not just dealing with a legal problem, you're dealing with a financial one that can end your business. Here's the stack I've built over 10 years.
From Danielle
"I remember the first time I had a patient call with a complaint about their results. I was terrified. The first thing I did was call my insurance broker. Knowing you have coverage is the difference between handling a situation calmly and panicking. Get properly insured before you need it."
The Core Insurance Policies

Professional Liability

Also called Malpractice Insurance

Covers claims arising from your professional services — a patient claims your Botox caused ptosis, a client says their lash extensions damaged their natural lashes, a filler patient alleges a complication. This is your most important coverage as a medical or esthetic provider.

Who needs it: every provider performing treatments

General Liability

Also called Slip & Fall / Premises Liability

Covers bodily injury or property damage that occurs at your practice — a client trips on a step, a patient's personal property is damaged. This is separate from professional liability and both are required. Most commercial leases require it as a condition of occupancy.

Who needs it: every practice with a physical location

Product Liability

Often bundled with GL or professional liability

Covers claims related to products you sell or use — skincare products, retail items, topical treatments. Verify whether your professional liability policy includes this or if it needs to be added. If you retail products, you need explicit product liability coverage.

Who needs it: anyone selling or applying products

Workers' Compensation

Required the moment you have employees

Covers your employees if they are injured on the job — a needlestick injury, a slip in the treatment room, a back injury from equipment. This is legally required in most states the moment you have even one W-2 employee. Check your state's specific threshold.

Who needs it: any practice with W-2 employees

Cyber Liability

Growing in importance — do not skip this

Covers data breaches, ransomware attacks, and HIPAA breach notification costs. If you store patient health information digitally — which you almost certainly do — a data breach triggers HIPAA notification requirements and potential fines. Cyber liability covers the response costs. Increasingly required by software vendors.

Who needs it: any practice storing digital PHI

Business Owner's Policy (BOP)

Bundles GL + property + business interruption

A BOP combines general liability, commercial property (your equipment, supplies, furniture), and business interruption insurance into one policy. Often more cost-effective than buying each separately. Ask your broker if a BOP makes sense for your practice size and service mix.

Who needs it: established practices with significant equipment
Questions to Ask Your Insurance Broker

Does my professional liability policy cover all the services I offer?Critical

Some policies exclude certain procedures — laser, IV therapy, injectables. List every service you perform and confirm each is covered explicitly. Exclusions matter.

Am I covered for employee actions under my policy?Critical

If an employee injures a patient, does your policy cover you as the employer? This is often a separate endorsement. Confirm before you hire anyone.

Is my policy occurrence-based or claims-made?

Occurrence-based covers incidents that happen during the policy period regardless of when the claim is filed. Claims-made only covers claims filed while the policy is active — you need tail coverage when you cancel.

What are my per-occurrence and aggregate limits?

Common: $1M per occurrence / $3M aggregate. Make sure these are adequate for your services. Injectables and laser treatments carry higher liability exposure.

Does my policy cover telemedicine or remote consultations?

If you conduct any virtual consultations or prescribe to patients you haven't seen in person, verify this is covered.

Danielle's insurance tip
"Work with a broker who specializes in medical spas or aesthetic practices — not a general commercial insurance agent. The policies are different. The exclusions are different. A general agent may not know to ask about your laser or your IV services. I use a specialty broker and I review my coverage every single year when it renews."
Med Spa Insurance Specialists to Research

Where to start your search

Search for brokers who specialize in: medical spa insurance, aesthetic practice insurance, medspa malpractice insurance. National carriers who frequently work with med spas include HPSO, Berxi, CM&F Group, Philadelphia Insurance, and Markel. The American Med Spa Association (AmSpa) also maintains an insurance resource directory for members. These are starting points — always compare multiple quotes and read the exclusions before you buy.

Section 03
Scope of Practice & State Licensing
The questions every med spa owner must answer — and where to find the answers for your specific state
This is the section that trips up more med spa owners than any other. Scope of practice — what you are legally allowed to do with your license — is governed by your state medical board, nursing board, or cosmetology board depending on your license type. And it varies wildly by state.

The most important thing I can tell you about scope of practice

Do not rely on what someone in a Facebook group tells you is legal in your state. Do not rely on what a trainer at a Botox course tells you. Do not rely on this guide. Call your state medical board directly, ask your question in writing, and get the answer in writing. Then have a healthcare attorney review it. This is the only way to know for certain what you are permitted to do.

The Framework Questions — Answer These for Your State

Who can perform Botox and dermal filler injections in my state?Critical

In some states only MDs and DOs may inject. In others, NPs, PAs, and RNs may inject. In some, trained estheticians may perform certain services under physician oversight. Know your state's exact rule — not a neighboring state's rule.

What level of physician oversight is required for medical services?Critical

Some states require on-site physician presence for every injection. Others allow physician oversight via collaborative practice agreement with the physician off-site. Others allow NP full practice authority with no physician required. Know your state.

What services require a medical license vs. an esthetic license in my state?Critical

Chemical peels, microneedling, laser treatments, and even some advanced facials fall in different regulatory categories by state. What an esthetician can do in Illinois may not be legal for an esthetician in Georgia.

Is my facility required to be licensed as a medical facility?

Some states require a med spa to obtain a clinic license or healthcare facility license in addition to a business license. Others do not. Check with your state health department.

What are my state's requirements for laser and IPL operation?

Some states require a physician on-site or on-call for laser treatments. Others allow NPs to operate lasers independently. Some require specific laser operator certifications. Check your state's radiation safety board as well as your medical board.

What are the supervision requirements for IV therapy in my state?

IV therapy regulation varies significantly. Some states require a physician to be on-site. Others allow NPs to run IV clinics with full practice authority. Confirm this specifically for your provider credential type.

Where to Find Your State's Rules

Your State Medical Board

The primary regulatory body for physicians, NPs, and PAs. Governs scope of practice for medical services including injectables, prescriptions, and supervision requirements. Search: "[Your State] Medical Board" or "[Your State] Board of Medicine."

Your State Nursing Board

Governs RN, NP, and LPN scope of practice including what NPs can do independently vs. under supervision. Critical if your provider is an NP. Search: "[Your State] Board of Nursing."

Your State Cosmetology / Esthetics Board

Governs esthetician scope of practice — what services require a medical license vs. can be performed with an esthetic license. Search: "[Your State] Department of Professional Regulation" or "cosmetology board."

Your State Department of Health

Governs facility licensing requirements — whether your med spa needs a clinic license, infection control inspections, or specific facility certifications beyond a standard business license.

From Danielle — Year 1 lesson
"The thing that kept me up at night in Year 1 was not knowing what I was allowed to do. I finally just called the Illinois Medical Board directly. They were actually helpful. They answered my questions, and I followed up in writing. Pick up the phone. Call your board. Get it in writing. It's not as scary as it sounds and it's worth every minute of awkwardness."
Section 04
Collaborative Practice Agreements
What a CPA is, when you need one, and what to look for — from someone who has one
A Collaborative Practice Agreement (CPA) — also called a Supervision Agreement, Collaborative Agreement, or Practice Agreement depending on your state — is a formal document between a licensed physician or advanced practice provider and a mid-level provider that defines the scope of collaborative practice. If you are an NP, PA, or RN running a med spa, this may be required in your state.
From Danielle
"Having Ryan Kent as my medical director and clinical partner changed everything at Hello Gorgeous. Not just for compliance — but for the quality of care we deliver. A CPA isn't just a piece of paper. It's a relationship. Find someone who actually believes in what you're building, not someone who just signs the form."
Key Questions About CPAs

Does my state require a CPA for my credential type?Check first

Some states have "full practice authority" for NPs — no CPA required. Others require physician collaboration for every prescription written. Your state nursing board website will list your state's NP practice authority level.

What must the CPA document include?Critical

A valid CPA typically includes: scope of services covered, supervision requirements (on-site vs. available by phone), prescribing authority, chart review requirements, emergency protocols, and termination procedures. Have a healthcare attorney draft or review yours.

How often must the CPA be reviewed and renewed?

Many states require annual review and re-signing of the CPA. Some require it to be filed with the state. Check your state's specific requirement — an expired CPA is the same as no CPA in a regulatory investigation.

What is the chart review requirement?

Most CPAs require the collaborating physician to review a percentage of patient charts at defined intervals — commonly 10–20% monthly. This must actually happen and be documented. Keep a log of every chart review meeting.

Is my collaborating physician adequately insured?

Your medical director should carry their own malpractice insurance that covers their supervisory role at your practice. Verify this. If something goes wrong, their coverage is part of your protection.

What to look for in a medical director / collaborating physician

  • Active license in your state — verify on your state medical board's website
  • Experience with aesthetic medicine, hormone therapy, or the services you offer
  • Malpractice coverage that includes the supervisory role
  • Genuine involvement in your practice — not just a signature on a document
  • Availability for emergency consultation — not just for routine chart review
  • An attorney-drafted CPA that both parties have reviewed and understand

"Medical Director for Hire" — What to Watch Out For

There are physicians who advertise "medical director" services and sign CPAs for dozens of practices simultaneously. Some are legitimate. Many are not. A medical director who doesn't know your protocols, hasn't visited your practice, and isn't reachable in an emergency is not protecting you — they're just cashing a check. Your CPA is only as protective as the relationship behind it.

Section 05
DEA Registration & Controlled Substances
What you need to know before you prescribe testosterone, GLP-1 medications, or any controlled substance
The moment your practice prescribes, dispenses, or stores controlled substances — including testosterone — you enter DEA territory. This is a federal layer of compliance that operates separately from your state licensing. It is not complicated once you understand the framework, but ignorance is not a defense.
DEA Registration Basics

Who must have a DEA number?Critical

Any prescriber who prescribes, dispenses, or administers Schedule II–V controlled substances must be DEA-registered. This includes testosterone (Schedule III). Apply at DEA.gov. Registration is per-location — a provider must have a DEA registration for each practice location where they prescribe.

What substances require DEA registration at a med spa?Know your list

Testosterone and anabolic steroids (Schedule III). Ketamine (Schedule III). Benzodiazepines (Schedule IV). Tramadol (Schedule IV). GLP-1 medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide are NOT controlled substances — no DEA required for those specifically. Verify every substance you prescribe.

Storage requirements for controlled substances

Must be stored in a substantially constructed locked safe or cabinet. Cannot be left accessible to unauthorized personnel. Separate from non-controlled medications when possible. Check DEA and state requirements for your specific situation.

Dispensing logs and record-keeping

Every dispensing of a controlled substance must be logged: patient name, date, quantity, lot number, prescriber signature. Logs must be maintained and available for DEA inspection. Biennial inventory required.

Theft or loss reporting

Any significant loss or theft of controlled substances must be reported to DEA and local law enforcement within one business day using DEA Form 106. Failure to report is a federal violation separate from the theft itself.

State CDS (Controlled Dangerous Substance) registration

Many states have their own controlled substance registration in addition to the federal DEA registration. Check whether your state requires a separate state CDS license for your prescribing provider.

From Danielle
"When we started offering testosterone replacement therapy, DEA registration was one of the first things we got in order. It sounds intimidating but the DEA website actually walks you through the application. The harder part is the ongoing compliance — the logs, the storage, the inventory. Build the system before you need it."
Section 06
HIPAA Compliance Baseline
The 12 things every practice must have in place from Day One
HIPAA applies to any practice that collects, stores, or transmits protected health information (PHI). If you take a client's name, date of birth, and a photo of their face — that is PHI. If you text a patient about their appointment — that is PHI in transit. HIPAA is not optional and it is not just for hospitals.
From Danielle
"HIPAA compliance is one of those things that feels overwhelming until you actually sit down and do it. It is not as complicated as people make it sound for a small practice. Most of it is common sense — protect your patients' information, document how you do it, and train your staff. That's the core of it."
The 12-Point HIPAA Baseline Checklist

Notice of Privacy Practices (NPP)Required Day 1

Every patient must receive your NPP at their first visit. It must explain how you use and protect their health information. Provide a copy and document that you offered it. Have a signed acknowledgment in every chart.

Written HIPAA Policies & ProceduresRequired

You must have documented policies covering how PHI is accessed, stored, transmitted, and disposed of. This doesn't need to be a 100-page manual — but it must exist in writing and be reviewed annually.

Staff HIPAA TrainingRequired — All Staff

Every member of your team who touches patient information must receive HIPAA training. Training must be documented. Retrain when policies change or when you hire new staff. Free training resources are available through HHS.gov.

Business Associate Agreements (BAAs)

Any vendor who accesses PHI on your behalf — your EHR, your billing company, your email marketing platform — must sign a BAA. This includes your booking software, your cloud storage provider, and your email platform if you send clinical communications through it.

Secure Electronic PHI Storage

PHI stored electronically must be protected with access controls, encryption, and audit logs. Your EHR system typically handles this — but verify with your vendor that it is HIPAA compliant and that you have a BAA in place.

Secure Messaging for PHI

Regular text messages and standard email are not HIPAA compliant for PHI. If you communicate clinical information with patients electronically, use a HIPAA-compliant messaging platform. Options include TigerConnect, Spruce Health, and others.

Before/After Photo Policy

Patient photos are PHI. You must have documented consent for clinical use (always) and separate consent for marketing use (optional). Your photo consent form must specify exactly how images will be stored, who can access them, and how they may be used.

Breach Response Plan

You must have a documented plan for responding to a data breach. HIPAA requires notification to affected patients within 60 days of discovering a breach. Breaches affecting 500+ individuals must also be reported to HHS and the media. Your cyber liability insurance covers the response costs.

Physical Safeguards

Paper charts must be stored securely and inaccessible to unauthorized individuals. Workstations with PHI must be positioned so they cannot be seen by other patients. Shred paper with PHI — don't trash it.

Patient Right of Access

Patients have the right to access their own records within 30 days of request. You must have a process for providing records when requested and must not charge more than a reasonable cost-based fee for copies.

Social Media Policy for PHI

Never post patient information, before/after photos, or anything that could identify a patient without their explicit written consent. This includes tagging, mentioning, or sharing any identifiable patient information in any format.

Annual HIPAA Risk Assessment

HIPAA requires covered entities to conduct an annual risk assessment — a review of potential vulnerabilities to PHI security. This can be done internally or with a HIPAA consultant. Document it and update your policies based on the findings.

HIPAA Resources — Free from HHS

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS.gov) provides free HIPAA training materials, policy templates, and guidance documents at hhs.gov/hipaa. The Office for Civil Rights (OCR) HIPAA portal is your primary federal resource. Many state health departments also offer HIPAA compliance guides specific to small practices.

Section 07
OSHA & Workplace Safety
The baseline requirements every practice must meet — starting from your first needle
OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) requirements apply to every employer with at least one employee. Even if you're a solo provider with a part-time receptionist, OSHA applies to you. The most relevant standards for med spas involve bloodborne pathogen protection — because you use needles.
OSHA Compliance Checklist for Med Spas

Bloodborne Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030)Required — All Practices

Applies to any workplace where employees may be exposed to blood or other potentially infectious materials. A written Exposure Control Plan is required, must be updated annually, and must be accessible to all employees.

Annual Bloodborne Pathogen TrainingRequired — All Staff

Every employee with potential bloodborne pathogen exposure must receive training at hire and annually thereafter. Training must be documented with date, content, and employee signature. Free training is available through OSHA and many state health departments.

Sharps Safety & DisposalRequired

Use FDA-cleared sharps containers. Never recap needles. Full sharps containers must be disposed of through a licensed medical waste disposal vendor — not in regular trash. Keep a disposal log.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Appropriate PPE — gloves, eye protection — must be provided by the employer at no cost to employees. Staff must be trained on proper PPE use and limitations. Gloves for all procedures involving blood or needles.

Hepatitis B Vaccination Offer

Employers must offer Hepatitis B vaccination to all employees with potential bloodborne pathogen exposure within 10 days of employment. Employees may decline with a signed declination form. Keep the offer and any declination documented.

Needlestick Injury Protocol

You must have a documented protocol for needlestick and sharps injuries. Staff must know exactly what to do if they experience a needlestick. Post-exposure evaluation must be available at no cost to the employee. Document every incident.

Medical Waste Disposal

Beyond sharps — biohazardous waste (blood-soaked materials, contaminated items) must be segregated in red biohazard bags and disposed of through a licensed medical waste vendor. Many providers use vendors like Stericycle or local medical waste services. Check your state's specific medical waste disposal regulations.

OSHA 300 Log (Injury & Illness Recordkeeping)

Employers with 10 or more employees must maintain OSHA Form 300 (Log of Work-Related Injuries and Illnesses). Smaller practices are partially exempt but must still report severe injuries (hospitalization, amputation, eye loss) to OSHA within 24 hours.

From Danielle
"OSHA was one of the things I didn't think about enough in my first year. Once I had staff handling needles, I realized I needed an Exposure Control Plan and bloodborne pathogen training. It's not complicated — it's just a binder, some training documentation, and the right sharps disposal vendor. Do it before you hire your first employee."
Section 08
Business Structure & Liability Protection
How to set up your business so a lawsuit doesn't take everything you own
Your business structure is your first line of legal defense. Operating as a sole proprietor means your personal assets — your house, your savings, your car — are exposed if your business is sued. Forming the right entity separates your personal finances from your business liability.
From Danielle
"I formed my LLC before I opened my doors. I'm not a lawyer and I'm not telling you what structure to use — that's a question for a business attorney who knows your situation. What I am telling you is: do not operate as a sole proprietor running needles through people's faces. Protect yourself."
Entity Types to Discuss With Your Attorney

LLC (Limited Liability Company)

Most common structure for small med spas and esthetic businesses. Separates personal assets from business liability. Flexible tax treatment. Relatively simple to form and maintain. Most states allow healthcare providers to form an LLC for business purposes separate from their professional license entity.

PLLC (Professional LLC)

Required in some states for licensed healthcare providers. Similar to an LLC but specifically for licensed professionals. Ask your state whether your license type requires a PLLC rather than a standard LLC for the clinical side of your practice.

PC or S-Corp

Professional Corporation or S-Corporation structures offer different tax advantages for higher-revenue practices. Typically more relevant once your practice is generating significant revenue. Discuss with your CPA and attorney when the time is right.

Liability Protection Checklist

Form your entity before you openDo this first

File with your state's Secretary of State office. Most states have online filing. Cost varies from $50–$500 depending on state. Use an attorney or a reputable service like LegalZoom as a starting point — but have an attorney review the operating agreement.

Maintain a separate business bank accountRequired for LLC protection

Commingling personal and business funds is the fastest way to pierce your LLC protection. Open a dedicated business checking account and keep all business transactions there. Never pay personal expenses from your business account.

Obtain an EIN (Employer Identification Number)

Apply for a free EIN through IRS.gov immediately after forming your entity. You need this to open a business bank account, hire employees, and file business taxes.

Register for state and local business licenses and permits

In addition to your professional license, your business typically needs a general business license from your city or county, a sales tax permit (if selling retail products), and potentially a health department permit depending on your services and location.

Create an operating agreement (LLC) or corporate bylaws (Corp)

This internal document defines how your business is governed — ownership percentages, decision-making processes, and what happens if a partner leaves. Critical if you have any business partners. Have an attorney draft this.

The Questions to Ask Before You Open
If I could go back to Day One and hand myself a list — this is it
From Danielle
"Here's the thing nobody tells you. You don't have to have all the answers before you open. You just have to know the right questions. The providers who get in trouble aren't the ones who didn't know — they're the ones who didn't ask. This list is every question I wish I'd had on Day One."
Your Pre-Opening Compliance Checklist

Have I confirmed scope of practice for every service I plan to offer in my state?Non-negotiable

Call your state medical board AND your state esthetics board. Ask specifically about each service. Get the answer documented.

Do I have the right business entity formed and active?Before Day 1

LLC, PLLC, or appropriate structure for your state and license type. Separate EIN. Separate business bank account.

Do I have professional liability insurance in place for every service I offer?Before Day 1

Confirm every treatment is covered. Confirm the policy limits. Know whether it's occurrence or claims-made.

Do I need a collaborative practice agreement for my credential type and state?Research first

Check your state NP practice authority. If you need one — get it drafted by a healthcare attorney before you offer any prescription or medical service.

Is my facility licensed for the services I plan to offer?

Check with your state health department. Some states require a clinic license for any practice performing injections.

Do I have a HIPAA Notice of Privacy Practices ready for my first patient?

Draft it before you open. Every first-visit patient gets a copy. Keep signed acknowledgments in every chart.

Do I have sharps containers and a licensed medical waste disposal vendor?

Set this up before you use your first needle. You cannot dispose of sharps in regular trash.

Do I have a written Bloodborne Pathogen Exposure Control Plan?

Required by OSHA the moment you have employees. Write it before you hire.

Have I consulted with a healthcare attorney about my specific situation?

Even a one-hour paid consultation with a healthcare attorney in your state is worth hundreds of times its cost in potential liability avoided. Do this.

Have I built my annual compliance review calendar?

Set calendar reminders for: annual insurance renewal review, CPA review and renewal, HIPAA annual risk assessment, OSHA annual training documentation, state license renewal dates.

Before you offer any medical service. Before you sign a lease for a clinical space. Before you hire your first employee. Before you sign a collaborative practice agreement.
