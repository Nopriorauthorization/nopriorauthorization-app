Section 01

Drug Suffix Decoder

Drug suffixes tell you the class before you even know the drug. Mastering these 10 suffixes lets you answer questions about unfamiliar drugs with confidence — the NCLEX tests this constantly.

-olol
Beta Blockers
metoprolol, atenolol, carvedilol. Slow HR and BP. Hold if HR <60 or SBP <90. Never stop abruptly — rebound HTN/angina. Masks hypoglycemia signs in diabetics.
-pril
ACE Inhibitors
lisinopril, enalapril, captopril. Classic SE = dry cough (switch to ARB). Risk: hyperkalemia, angioedema, teratogenic. Avoid in pregnancy. First-line for HTN + HF + DM.
-sartan
ARBs
losartan, valsartan, irbesartan. Same as ACEi but NO cough. Still hyperkalemia + renal risk. Teratogenic — contraindicated in pregnancy.
-statin
HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors
atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin. Give at night (cholesterol made at night). Avoid grapefruit. SE: myopathy → rhabdomyolysis. Check CK if muscle pain reported.
-dipine
Calcium Channel Blockers (dihydropyridine)
amlodipine, nifedipine, felodipine. For HTN and angina. SE: flushing, peripheral edema, reflex tachycardia. Avoid grapefruit. No effect on heart rate (unlike non-DHP CCBs).
-mycin / -cin
Antibiotics (various classes)
erythromycin, vancomycin, gentamicin. Vancomycin: monitor troughs (10–20 mcg/mL), "Red Man Syndrome" with rapid infusion — slow the rate. Aminoglycosides: ototoxic + nephrotoxic.
-floxacin
Fluoroquinolones
ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin. Broad-spectrum antibiotics. Risk: tendon rupture (especially Achilles), contraindicated <18 yrs, avoid in pregnancy. Phototoxicity — sun protection needed.
-mab
Monoclonal Antibodies
rituximab, trastuzumab, adalimumab. Biologic/immunotherapy agents. High infusion reaction risk — premedicating with diphenhydramine and acetaminophen. Monitor for infection (immunosuppressive).
-tidine
H2 Blockers
famotidine, ranitidine (withdrawn), cimetidine. Reduce gastric acid via histamine-2 receptor blockade. Less potent than PPIs. Used for GERD, peptic ulcer disease.
-prazole
Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)
omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole. Strongest acid suppression. Give 30–60 min before first meal. Long-term use: risk of hypomagnesemia, C. diff, fractures.

Section 02

High-Alert Medications

High-alert medications have a heightened risk of causing significant patient harm when used in error. The ISMP list is the standard. Know these 8 categories — the NCLEX tests each one.

InsulinDouble-check dose with a second RN. Never abbreviate "U" (write "units" — U looks like 0). Only use insulin syringes. Regular insulin is the only type given IV. Glargine/Lantus has NO peak — once daily. Lispro is rapid-acting — give WITH meal.
HeparinWeight-based dosing — always double check. Monitor aPTT (therapeutic: 46–70 sec). Antidote: protamine sulfate. Watch for HIT (Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia) — paradoxical clotting, drop in platelets. Switch to argatroban if HIT suspected.
WarfarinMonitor INR every visit. Therapeutic INR: 2–3 (standard), 2.5–3.5 (mechanical heart valve). Antidote: Vitamin K (slow), FFP (fast). Many drug and food interactions. Leafy greens ↑ Vitamin K → ↓ drug effect. Teach consistency, not elimination, of greens.
OpioidsAssess RR before every dose — hold if RR <12. Have Naloxone (Narcan) accessible. Risk: respiratory depression, constipation, sedation, tolerance. Rotate sites (patches). Do NOT crush extended-release formulations.
DigoxinCheck apical pulse for 1 full minute — hold if <60. Therapeutic level: 0.5–2 ng/mL. Toxicity signs: nausea, visual changes (yellow-green halos), bradycardia. K+ must be normal — hypokalemia potentiates toxicity. Antidote: Digibind (Digoxin Immune Fab).
Concentrated ElectrolytesNever give concentrated KCl or NaCl IV push. Always dilute. Must be stored separately on restricted-access shelving. Administration requires two-nurse verification at most facilities.
ChemotherapyTriple-check drug, dose, route, patient, and cycle. PPE required during preparation and administration. Watch for extravasation — vesicant drugs cause tissue necrosis. Know the facility extravasation protocol before administering.
NMBAsNeuromuscular blocking agents — patient is awake but paralyzed. Must have ventilator ready before administering. Always concurrent with sedation and analgesia. Never confused with similar-appearing vials.
🚨
Always Check 6 Rights
Right patient (2 identifiers) · Right drug · Right dose · Right route · Right time · Right documentation. For high-alert meds, add: Right indication and Right response monitoring.

Antidotes — Know These Cold

The NCLEX tests antidotes regularly, often buried in clinical scenarios. Memorize every pair on this list. In practice, knowing the antidote could be the difference between life and death.

Section 04

Therapeutic Drug Levels

These drugs have narrow therapeutic indices — too little = ineffective, too much = toxic. The NCLEX tests what toxicity looks like and what to do about it.

📊 Therapeutic Level Reference
DrugTherapeutic LevelToxic SignsAction if Toxic
Digoxin0.5–2 ng/mLN/V, bradycardia, yellow-green halos, dysrhythmiaHold, check K+, notify, Digibind if severe
Lithium0.6–1.2 mEq/L>1.5 = tremor, confusion; >2.0 = seizures, comaHold, hydrate, dialysis if severe
Phenytoin (Dilantin)10–20 mcg/mLNystagmus, ataxia, slurred speech, confusionHold, check level, reduce dose
Theophylline10–20 mcg/mLTachycardia, N/V, tremors, seizuresHold, check level, supportive care
Vancomycin (trough)10–20 mcg/mLRed man syndrome, nephrotoxicity, ototoxicitySlow infusion, monitor renal function
Warfarin (INR)2.0–3.0 (standard)Bleeding — GI, intracranial, hematuriaHold, Vit K or FFP per order
Heparin (aPTT)46–70 sec (therapeutic)Bleeding, HIT (platelet drop)Stop infusion, protamine, monitor
💡
Lithium — Special Considerations
Lithium is excreted by the kidneys and competes with sodium. Low sodium diet or dehydration = higher lithium levels = toxicity risk. Teach patients to maintain consistent salt intake and stay hydrated. Avoid NSAIDs and ACE inhibitors (both raise lithium levels).

Section 05

Nursing Considerations by Drug Class

These are the before-you-give, while-you-give, and after-you-give standards for the most tested drug classes. This is where clinical judgment meets pharmacology on the NCLEX.

Beta BlockersBefore: Check HR (hold if <60) and SBP (hold if <90). During: Monitor for bradycardia, hypotension, bronchospasm. Teach: Never stop abruptly — can cause rebound HTN/MI. Masks hypoglycemia symptoms in diabetics.
Loop DiureticsBefore: Check electrolytes (especially K+), weight, BP. During: Monitor I&O, daily weights, K+. Teach: Take in AM to avoid nocturia. High-dose IV furosemide: risk of ototoxicity. "Furosemide causes furious urination."
ACE InhibitorsBefore: Check renal function, K+. During: Monitor BP, creatinine, K+. Teach: Dry cough = common SE — can switch to ARB. Report facial swelling (angioedema) immediately — stop drug. Teratogenic — not for pregnancy.
Steroids (Corticosteroids)During: Monitor blood glucose (↑), BP, Na+, signs of infection (mask fever). Teach: Never stop abruptly — taper. Cushingoid effects: moon face, buffalo hump, central obesity, striae. Suppresses immune system — infection risk.
AminoglycosidesBefore: Check renal function (BUN/Cr), baseline hearing. During: Monitor peak and trough levels, urine output, daily BUN/Cr. Avoid combining with other nephrotoxic or ototoxic drugs. Trough drawn 30 min before next dose.
SSRIs / SNRIsTeach: Takes 2–4 weeks to feel full effect — do NOT stop early. Serotonin syndrome risk when combined with MAOIs or other serotonergic agents (fever, agitation, tremors, diarrhea, clonus). Never combine with MAOIs — 14-day washout required.

Section 06

High-Yield NCLEX Questions

Ten pharmacology scenarios that appear frequently on the NCLEX. Think through each one before reading the answer.

Q1A patient on Metformin is scheduled for a CT scan with contrast dye tomorrow. What is the priority nursing action?
Hold Metformin 48 hours before AND after the contrast procedure. Contrast dye + Metformin = risk of lactic acidosis due to renal stress. Notify provider and radiology. Resume only after renal function is confirmed normal post-scan.
Q2A patient is prescribed an ACE inhibitor AND a K+-sparing diuretic (spironolactone). What does the nurse monitor closely?
Potassium — hyperkalemia risk. Both drugs raise K+ independently. Together, they significantly increase the risk of dangerous hyperkalemia. Monitor K+ frequently and educate patient to avoid K+ supplements and high-K+ foods.
Q3A patient on an MAOI eats aged cheddar cheese at dinner and develops a severe headache and BP of 210/120. What happened?
Tyramine-induced hypertensive crisis. MAOIs block monoamine oxidase, which normally breaks down tyramine in foods. Tyramine accumulates → massive catecholamine release → severe HTN. Teach: avoid aged cheeses, wine, cured meats, fermented foods.
Q4Which insulin can be given intravenously?
Regular insulin ONLY. All other insulin types — NPH, glargine, detemir, lispro, aspart — must NEVER be given IV. Regular insulin is clear; NPH is cloudy. When mixing, draw up clear (regular) before cloudy (NPH).
Q5A patient on Atorvastatin reports new onset muscle pain and weakness. What should the nurse do?
Hold the statin and check CK (creatine kinase) level immediately. Muscle pain on statins can indicate myopathy or rhabdomyolysis (breakdown of muscle tissue → acute kidney injury). Notify provider. Resume only with provider approval after CK normalizes.
Q6A patient is found unresponsive with RR of 6 and pinpoint pupils. They are on PCA morphine. What is the priority?
Administer Naloxone (Narcan) immediately. Pinpoint pupils + respiratory depression + opioid on board = opioid overdose. Give Naloxone 0.4mg IV/IM/IN, position patient, apply O2, call rapid response. Be prepared to repeat — Narcan wears off faster than most opioids.
Q7Which drugs are teratogenic and must be avoided in pregnancy?
ACE inhibitors, ARBs, Warfarin, Tetracyclines, Fluoroquinolones, Isotretinoin, Methotrexate, NSAIDs (3rd trimester), Lithium, Valproic acid. On NCLEX, if a woman of childbearing age is prescribed any of these, confirm contraception use or hold/discuss the prescription.
Q8A patient receiving IV Vancomycin suddenly develops flushing, redness, and itching across the face and upper body. What should the nurse do?
Red Man Syndrome — slow the infusion rate immediately. This is an infusion-related reaction (not a true allergy) caused by vancomycin infused too rapidly. Slow to infuse over at least 60 minutes. Diphenhydramine may be given. This is NOT an indication to stop permanently.
Q9A patient with asthma is prescribed Metoprolol for hypertension. What should the nurse do?
Question the order — beta blockers are contraindicated in asthma. Beta blockers block beta-2 receptors in the lungs → bronchoconstriction → can trigger asthma attack. Notify provider. A CCB or ACEi may be safer alternatives for this patient.
Q10A patient's Digoxin level is 2.4 ng/mL. They are also reporting nausea and seeing yellow halos around lights. What is the priority?
Hold Digoxin, check K+, and notify provider immediately. Digoxin level >2.0 = toxic. Visual disturbances (halos, color changes) are classic toxicity signs. Hypokalemia worsens toxicity — correct K+ first. Severe toxicity → Digibind. Continuous cardiac monitoring.
