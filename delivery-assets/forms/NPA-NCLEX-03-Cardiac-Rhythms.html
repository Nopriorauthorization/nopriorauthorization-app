Section 01

EKG Components — Normal Values

Before interpreting rhythms, you must know what normal looks like. Every deviation from these values tells a story — and the NCLEX tests whether you can read it.

🔢 Normal EKG Values
Wave / IntervalNormal RangeWhat It RepresentsAbnormal Means
P Wave0.06–0.12 secAtrial depolarizationAbsent = no atrial activity (A-Fib, junctional)
PR Interval0.12–0.20 secAV node conduction delay>0.20 = heart block; short = pre-excitation
QRS Complex<0.12 sec (3 small boxes)Ventricular depolarization>0.12 = bundle branch block or ventricular origin
QT Interval<0.44 sec (corrected)Total ventricular activityProlonged = torsades de pointes risk
T WaveUpright in most leadsVentricular repolarizationPeaked = hyperkalemia; inverted = ischemia
ST SegmentIsoelectric (flat)Between depol. and repol.Elevated = STEMI; depressed = ischemia/NSTEMI
Heart Rate60–100 bpmVentricular rate<60 = brady; >100 = tachy
💡
Quick HR Calculation
300 ÷ number of large boxes between R waves = HR in bpm. If R waves are 2 large boxes apart: 300 ÷ 2 = 150 bpm. If 5 boxes apart: 300 ÷ 5 = 60 bpm. Only works for regular rhythms.

Section 02

7-Step Strip Interpretation Method

Use this systematic approach every time. The NCLEX rewards nurses who have a process — and penalizes guessing based on one feature. Work through all 7 steps.

Step 1Rate: Count it. Is it 60–100 (normal)? <60 = bradycardia. >100 = tachycardia. Use the 300-box method or count 6-second strip × 10.
Step 2Rhythm: Regular or irregular? Measure R-R intervals across the strip. If they're the same = regular. Variable = irregular. Irregularly irregular = A-Fib.
Step 3P Waves: Are there P waves? Are they upright? Is there one P before every QRS? Are all P waves the same shape? No P waves = A-Fib or junctional.
Step 4PR Interval: Measure it. 0.12–0.20 sec is normal. >0.20 = 1st degree block. Progressively lengthening = Wenckebach. No relationship = 3rd degree block.
Step 5QRS Width: <0.12 sec = narrow = supraventricular origin (atria/AV node). >0.12 sec = wide = ventricular origin or bundle branch block.
Step 6ST Segment: Flat = normal. Elevated ≥1mm in ≥2 contiguous leads = STEMI. Depressed = ischemia or NSTEMI. Also check T wave for peaking or inversion.
Step 7Name It & Act: Identify the rhythm. Then — most importantly — assess the patient. Treat the patient, not the monitor. Symptoms + strip together drive your intervention.
🚨
Critical Rule: Assess the Patient First
A sleeping patient in A-Fib is different from an unconscious patient in A-Fib. A rhythm on the monitor never overrides clinical assessment. Always check: Is the patient responsive? Do they have a pulse? Are they symptomatic?

Section 03

Life-Threatening Rhythms

These rhythms require immediate action. Know the strip characteristics, the intervention, and why. The NCLEX expects you to prioritize correctly and act fast.

Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)
Chaotic, wavy baseline — no identifiable waves. NO PULSE. Pulseless cardiac arrest. Action: Call code, start CPR, DEFIBRILLATE (unsynchronized shock) immediately. AED or defibrillator. Epinephrine and amiodarone per ACLS after initial shocks. Not shockable → check leads.
Ventricular Tachycardia (VT) — with or without pulse
Wide, bizarre QRS complexes >0.12 sec at rate 100–250 bpm. P waves absent or dissociated. WITH pulse (stable): Amiodarone IV, synchronized cardioversion. WITHOUT pulse: Treat as VF — CPR + defibrillation. Key distinction: pulseless VT = same treatment as VF.
Asystole ("Flatline")
Completely flat line — no electrical activity. Check two leads to confirm — rule out lead disconnection. NOT shockable. Action: CPR + epinephrine every 3–5 min. Identify and treat reversible causes (5 H's and 5 T's). Worst prognosis of pulseless rhythms.
Torsades de Pointes
Polymorphic VT — QRS complexes twist around the baseline like a ribbon. Associated with prolonged QT interval. Causes: hypomagnesemia, hypokalemia, drugs (quinidine, haloperidol, amiodarone). Treatment: Magnesium sulfate IV. If pulseless → defibrillate.
Complete (3rd Degree) Heart Block
P waves and QRS complexes march independently — no relationship. Atria and ventricles beating at their own rates. Rate typically 20–40 bpm (ventricular escape). Action: Atropine (temporary), transcutaneous pacing → permanent pacemaker. This is a true cardiac emergency.
⚡
Defib vs Cardioversion — The Key Difference
Defibrillation = unsynchronized shock → pulseless rhythms (VF, pulseless VT, asystole — though asystole not shockable). Cardioversion = synchronized shock (timed to QRS) → rhythms WITH a pulse (A-Fib, A-Flutter, stable VT). Getting this backwards on the NCLEX = wrong answer.

Section 04

Common Non-Lethal Rhythms

These rhythms are common on med-surg and telemetry floors. You need to recognize them, understand when they become dangerous, and know your first-line response.

Normal Sinus Rhythm (NSR)
Rate 60–100, regular, one P before every QRS, PR 0.12–0.20, QRS <0.12. This is your goal rhythm — nothing to do except document.
Sinus Bradycardia
Rate <60, otherwise normal P-QRS relationship. Common in athletes, sleep, vagal stimulation, beta blockers. Treat only if symptomatic (dizziness, hypotension, syncope). First line: Atropine 0.5mg IV. If refractory: transcutaneous pacing.
Sinus Tachycardia
Rate >100, normal morphology. Never a primary rhythm — always caused by something else: fever, pain, anxiety, dehydration, hemorrhage, hypoxia, HF. Treat the CAUSE, not the rate. Do not give rate control medications without identifying the trigger.
Atrial Fibrillation (A-Fib)
No P waves — replaced by chaotic fibrillatory activity. Irregularly irregular ventricular response. Most common sustained dysrhythmia. Risks: clot formation in atria → stroke (anticoagulate!). Management: rate control (beta blockers, digoxin, diltiazem) + anticoagulation (Warfarin, DOACs) ± cardioversion.
Atrial Flutter
Sawtooth P waves (flutter waves) at ~300/min. Ventricular rate typically 150 (2:1 block) or 75 (4:1 block). Regular. Management: rate control, cardioversion, ablation. Also carries clot/stroke risk — anticoagulate if >48 hours or unknown duration.
PVCs (Premature Ventricular Contractions)
Early, wide (>0.12 sec), bizarre QRS with no P wave. Followed by compensatory pause. Occasional PVCs = benign (common in stress, caffeine, electrolyte imbalances). Concerning: >6/min, multifocal, couplets, or R-on-T phenomenon → notify provider, check electrolytes (especially K+ and Mg2+).

Section 05

Heart Blocks — 1st, 2nd, 3rd Degree

Heart blocks are disturbances in conduction from the atria to the ventricles through the AV node. The key differences lie in the PR interval behavior and whether QRS complexes are dropped.

📊 Heart Block Comparison
Block TypePR IntervalDropped QRS?Danger LevelTreatment
1st Degree>0.20 sec, constantNoLow — benignMonitor only, no treatment
2nd Degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach)Progressively lengthening until QRS dropsYes, periodicallyLow-moderateMonitor; atropine if symptomatic
2nd Degree Mobitz IIConstant, then suddenly drops a QRS (no warning)Yes, unpredictablyHIGH — can progress to 3rd degreePacemaker — do not wait
3rd Degree (Complete)No relationship — P and QRS independentEffectively yes — complete dissociationCRITICALEmergency transcutaneous pacing → permanent pacemaker
⚠️
Mobitz II = Danger. Always.
Mobitz II can progress to complete heart block without warning. Unlike Wenckebach (which gives you a pattern), Mobitz II drops beats unpredictably. Pacemaker is always the intervention — never just monitor and wait. This is one of the most tested block distinctions on NCLEX.

Section 06

STEMI Localization by EKG Leads

ST elevation in specific leads tells you which wall of the heart is affected — and which artery is occluded. This is clinically critical and tested on the NCLEX.

📍 STEMI Territory Map
Leads with ST ElevationWall AffectedArtery (Most Likely)Key Clinical Note
II, III, aVFInferiorRCA (Right Coronary Artery)Avoid nitrates — preload-dependent. Risk: AV block, bradycardia.
V1–V4AnteriorLAD (Left Anterior Descending)Largest area at risk. Highest mortality. Watch for HF, cardiogenic shock.
I, aVL, V5–V6LateralLCx (Left Circumflex)Often occurs with anterior or inferior MI.
V1–V2 (reciprocal ST depression + R>S)PosteriorRCA or LCxFlip the ECG or use posterior leads V7–V9 to confirm.
V4R (right-sided leads)Right VentricleRCA (proximal)Use right-sided leads to confirm. Avoid nitrates, diuretics. Give fluids.
💊
Inferior MI + Nitrates = Dangerous Drop
Inferior MI often involves the right ventricle. The RV depends on preload (volume) to maintain output. Nitrates cause vasodilation → preload drops → BP crashes → cardiogenic shock. Always evaluate for RV involvement before giving nitrates in inferior MI.

Section 07

Antiarrhythmic Drug Reference

Know the primary use, key nursing consideration, and one major side effect for each of these drugs. The NCLEX regularly tests antiarrhythmic selection and monitoring.

AmiodaroneUses: VT, VF, A-Fib. Key SE: Pulmonary toxicity (check CXR/PFTs), thyroid dysfunction (contains iodine), photosensitivity, hepatotoxicity. Very long half-life (40–55 days). Monitor thyroid and liver function chronically.
AdenosineUse: SVT (narrow complex only). Terminates re-entry circuits. Give rapidly IV push followed by a flush — half-life is <10 seconds. Warn patient of brief chest pressure/flushing/feeling of doom (normal and short-lived). Contraindicated in 2nd/3rd degree heart block.
AtropineUse: Symptomatic bradycardia. First-line drug for unstable bradycardia. Dose: 0.5mg IV every 3–5 min, max 3mg. Anticholinergic: tachycardia, dry mouth, urinary retention, dilated pupils. May not work in heart transplant patients (no vagal innervation).
DigoxinUses: Rate control in A-Fib, heart failure. Slows AV conduction. Check apical pulse 1 full minute before giving — hold if <60. Therapeutic level 0.5–2 ng/mL. Toxicity signs: N/V, bradycardia, visual halos. K+ must be normal. Antidote: Digibind.
Magnesium SulfateUses: Torsades de pointes, hypomagnesemia, eclampsia. Monitor: RR (hold if <12), DTRs (loss = early toxicity), urine output (<30 mL/hr = hold). Antidote always at bedside: Calcium gluconate 10% IV.
LidocaineUse: Ventricular dysrhythmias (especially post-MI PVCs). Given IV only in cardiac setting. CNS toxicity at high doses: confusion, tremors, seizures. Monitor for these signs. Less commonly used now that amiodarone is preferred.

Section 08

High-Yield NCLEX Questions

Q1A patient goes into VF on the monitor. What is the nurse's priority action?
Call a code and defibrillate (unsynchronized shock) immediately while starting CPR. VF = pulseless = no cardiac output. Do not delay. Every second without defibrillation in VF reduces survival by ~10%. Do not cardiovert — cardioversion is for rhythms WITH a pulse.
Q2A patient in A-Fib asks why they need blood thinners. How should the nurse explain?
When the atria quiver instead of contract, blood pools and can form clots. If a clot travels to the brain, it causes a stroke. Anticoagulation prevents clot formation. This is the #1 cause of AF-related morbidity. Simple, clear education = NCLEX correct answer.
Q3A patient's monitor shows peaked T waves and the K+ came back at 6.8 mEq/L. What is the priority?
Notify the provider immediately — this is a hyperkalemic cardiac emergency. Prepare calcium gluconate (stabilizes the cardiac membrane), insulin + dextrose (shifts K+ into cells), sodium bicarbonate, and kayexalate or dialysis for removal. Continuous cardiac monitoring.
Q4What is the difference between defibrillation and synchronized cardioversion?
Defibrillation: unsynchronized shock, used for pulseless rhythms (VF, pulseless VT). Delivers energy at any point in the cardiac cycle. Cardioversion: synchronized shock timed to the QRS, used for rhythms WITH a pulse (A-Fib, A-Flutter, stable VT). Getting these backwards on the NCLEX = automatic wrong answer.
Q5A patient is in stable VT with a pulse and BP of 110/70. What is the appropriate treatment?
Stable VT: Amiodarone IV (first-line) or synchronized cardioversion. The patient is stable (has a pulse, BP adequate) so you have time to try medication first. If BP drops or patient becomes unconscious → cardiovert immediately. Never give Adenosine for wide-complex VT.
Q6ST elevation in leads II, III, and aVF. What territory and what precaution?
Inferior STEMI — RCA occlusion. Avoid nitrates (RV may be involved — preload-dependent). Obtain right-sided leads (V4R) to assess for RV involvement. If RV infarction confirmed: give IV fluids, avoid diuretics, nitrates, and morphine. Notify cath lab.
Q7A patient is on amiodarone long-term. What should the nurse monitor?
Pulmonary function (dyspnea, new cough = pulmonary toxicity), thyroid function (hyper or hypo), liver function (AST/ALT), and vision (optic neuropathy). Also teach photosensitivity — sunscreen required. Amiodarone has more systemic effects than almost any other cardiac drug.
Q8A patient develops torsades de pointes. Their Mg level is 1.0 mEq/L. What is the treatment?
Magnesium sulfate IV — immediately. Hypomagnesemia causes prolonged QT → torsades. Give Mag sulfate 1–2g IV over 10–20 min. If pulseless → defibrillate. Always correct electrolytes (K+, Mg2+) to prevent recurrence.
Q9A patient's PR interval progressively lengthens then a QRS drops, then the cycle resets. What is this rhythm?
2nd Degree Heart Block Mobitz I (Wenckebach). This is the more benign of the two Mobitz types — the AV node fatigues with each beat until it drops one, then resets. Usually asymptomatic. Monitor; treat only if symptomatic. Does not typically require pacing.
Q10A patient in A-Fib for 3 days is scheduled for cardioversion. What must happen first?
Rule out atrial thrombus before cardioversion. If A-Fib has been present >48 hours (or unknown duration), a transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) must be done to check for clot in the left atrial appendage — OR the patient must be anticoagulated for at least 3 weeks before cardioversion. Cardioverting without this → stroke risk.
