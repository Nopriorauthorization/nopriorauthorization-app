Section 02

The ROME Method

ROME is the most widely taught and tested ABG interpretation method. Once you internalize it, you can interpret any ABG question in under 30 seconds.

🔑
ROME = Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal
Respiratory = Opposite: The pH and PaCO2 move in OPPOSITE directions.
   ↓pH + ↑CO2 = Respiratory ACIDOSIS
   ↑pH + ↓CO2 = Respiratory ALKALOSIS

Metabolic = Equal: The pH and HCO3- move in the SAME direction.
   ↓pH + ↓HCO3- = Metabolic ACIDOSIS
   ↑pH + ↑HCO3- = Metabolic ALKALOSIS
📊 4-Step ABG Interpretation Process
Step 1
Check the pH. Is it <7.35 (acidosis), 7.35–7.45 (normal), or >7.45 (alkalosis)?
Step 2
Check PaCO2 (Respiratory OPPOSITE). Does it match pH direction? If pH↓ and CO2↑ → respiratory. If pH↓ and CO2 normal → go to metabolic.
Step 3
Check HCO3- (Metabolic EQUAL). Does it match pH direction? If pH↓ and HCO3↓ → metabolic acidosis.
Step 4
Check for compensation. If both CO2 AND HCO3 are abnormal → compensation is occurring. If pH is still abnormal = partially compensated. If pH is normal = fully compensated.

Section 03

Respiratory Acidosis

Respiratory acidosis occurs when the lungs can't remove enough CO2 — it accumulates, forming carbonic acid, which drives the pH down. Think: hypoventilation.

Respiratory Acidosis
pH <7.35 + PaCO2 >45 mmHg
Pathophysiology: CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 (carbonic acid). When CO2 builds up (lungs not blowing it off), acid accumulates → pH drops.

Common Causes: COPD exacerbation, severe asthma attack, pneumonia, pneumothorax, airway obstruction, CNS depression (opioids, anesthesia, head injury, sedatives), neuromuscular disorders (Guillain-Barré, myasthenia gravis), obesity hypoventilation syndrome.

Signs and Symptoms: Headache, confusion, lethargy, cyanosis, tachycardia, tachypnea (compensating), respiratory muscle fatigue, CO2 narcosis (in severe COPD — high CO2 causes CNS depression).

Compensation: Kidneys retain HCO3- (takes hours to days). HCO3 rises to buffer the excess acid.

Treatment: Improve ventilation — bronchodilators, positioning (HOB up), supplemental O2 (caution in COPD — use 1–2L NC, avoid high-flow O2 which removes hypoxic drive), mechanical ventilation if severe.
⚠️
COPD + O2 = Caution
COPD patients rely on hypoxic drive (low O2) to breathe — their chemoreceptors have adapted to high CO2. Giving high-flow O2 removes the only remaining respiratory stimulus → respiratory depression → worsening acidosis. Use 1–2L NC and titrate carefully. Target SpO2 88–92% in COPD.
🌬️
Section 04

Respiratory Alkalosis

Respiratory alkalosis occurs when the lungs blow off too much CO2 — removing too much acid raises the pH. Think: hyperventilation.

Respiratory Alkalosis
pH >7.45 + PaCO2 <35 mmHg
Pathophysiology: Hyperventilation → excessive CO2 blown off → less acid → pH rises.

Common Causes: Anxiety/panic attack, pain, fever, sepsis (early), pregnancy, high altitude, pulmonary embolism (tachypnea), mechanical ventilation (rate/tidal volume too high), hyperventilation syndrome.

Signs and Symptoms: Lightheadedness, perioral tingling, fingertip tingling (paresthesias), tetany, palpitations, anxiety, carpopedal spasm. Symptoms occur because low CO2 → cerebral vasoconstriction.

Compensation: Kidneys excrete HCO3- (takes hours to days). HCO3 drops to reduce the base.

Treatment: Slow breathing, treat underlying cause (anxiety, pain, fever), breathe into paper bag (increases CO2), adjust ventilator settings if mechanically ventilated.

Section 05

Metabolic Acidosis

Metabolic acidosis happens when there's too much acid or too little bicarbonate in the body — a problem originating in the metabolism or kidneys, not the lungs.

Metabolic Acidosis
pH <7.35 + HCO3- <22 mEq/L
Pathophysiology: Excess acid accumulation or loss of bicarbonate → pH drops. The kidneys cannot excrete enough acid or reclaim enough HCO3-.

Common Causes: Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) — ketone body accumulation, lactic acidosis (sepsis, shock, hypoxia), acute/chronic renal failure — can't excrete H+, diarrhea — loss of HCO3- in stool, aspirin overdose, methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning, starvation.

Signs and Symptoms: Kussmaul respirations (deep, rapid, labored — the lungs trying to blow off CO2 to compensate), fruity breath (DKA), lethargy, headache, nausea/vomiting, cardiac dysrhythmias (if severe), abdominal pain.

Compensation: Lungs hyperventilate to blow off CO2 (fast — within minutes). PaCO2 drops.

Treatment: Treat the underlying cause. DKA → insulin drip + IV fluids + electrolyte replacement. Renal failure → dialysis. Bicarb therapy only for severe, life-threatening acidosis (pH <7.1).
🌬️
Kussmaul Respirations = Metabolic Acidosis Compensation
Kussmaul breathing is deep, rapid, and labored — it looks like the patient is working hard to breathe even when they aren't in respiratory distress. This is the body's way of blowing off CO2 to raise pH. Classic presentation of DKA. On the NCLEX: "deep, rapid breathing" in a diabetic patient = think metabolic acidosis → check glucose and ketones.

Section 06

Metabolic Alkalosis

Metabolic alkalosis happens when there's too much base or too little acid in the body — most commonly from loss of gastric acid through vomiting or NG suction.

Metabolic Alkalosis
pH >7.45 + HCO3- >26 mEq/L
Pathophysiology: Loss of H+ (acid) or gain of HCO3- → pH rises. The most common mechanism is losing gastric acid (HCl) through vomiting or NG suction.

Common Causes: Prolonged vomiting, nasogastric (NG) suctioning — both remove HCl, loop and thiazide diuretics — cause K+ and Cl- loss → metabolic alkalosis, excessive antacid use (calcium carbonate), hypokalemia (K+ shifts extracellularly, H+ moves into cells), hyperaldosteronism.

Signs and Symptoms: Confusion, irritability, muscle weakness, cramping, tetany, nausea, hypoventilation (body retaining CO2 to lower pH), cardiac dysrhythmias.

Compensation: Lungs hypoventilate (retain CO2). CO2 rises to buffer the excess base — but compensation is limited because the body won't allow O2 to drop dangerously low.

Treatment: Replace K+ and Cl- (K+ depletion almost always accompanies this). Treat cause. Stop NG suctioning if possible, administer antiemetics. Replace fluids. Acetazolamide (Diamox) promotes HCO3- excretion in severe cases.
💡
Vomiting vs. Diarrhea — Opposite Effects
Vomiting → lose gastric acid (HCl) → lose H+ → pH rises → metabolic alkalosis.
Diarrhea → lose intestinal secretions rich in HCO3- → lose base → pH drops → metabolic acidosis.
This distinction appears frequently on the NCLEX. The stomach contains acid; the intestines contain base.

Section 07

Compensation — The Body's Response

When one system is dysregulated, the other compensates. Understanding compensation helps you determine if a disorder is new (uncompensated) or ongoing (partially or fully compensated).

📊 Compensation Summary
DisorderPrimary ProblemCompensating SystemCompensation ResponseSpeed
Resp Acidosis↑CO2KidneysRetain HCO3- (↑HCO3)Hours–days
Resp Alkalosis↓CO2KidneysExcrete HCO3- (↓HCO3)Hours–days
Met Acidosis↓HCO3-LungsHyperventilate (↓CO2)Minutes
Met Alkalosis↑HCO3-LungsHypoventilate (↑CO2)Minutes
📐
How to Determine Compensation Level
Uncompensated: pH abnormal, one value (CO2 or HCO3) abnormal, the other normal — compensation hasn't started or just began.
Partially compensated: pH still abnormal, both CO2 and HCO3 are abnormal — compensation is occurring but pH not yet normalized.
Fully compensated: pH normal, both CO2 and HCO3 are abnormal — compensation has restored pH to normal range.

Section 08

High-Yield NCLEX Questions

Q1ABG results: pH 7.28, PaCO2 58, HCO3 24. What is the interpretation?
Respiratory Acidosis, uncompensated. pH <7.35 = acidosis. CO2 >45 and moving opposite to pH = respiratory cause. HCO3 is still normal (24) = no compensation yet. Think: COPD, hypoventilation, opioid overdose.
Q2ABG: pH 7.50, PaCO2 30, HCO3 23. What is the interpretation?
Respiratory Alkalosis, uncompensated. pH >7.45 = alkalosis. CO2 <35 and moving opposite to pH = respiratory. HCO3 normal = no compensation. Think: anxiety, hyperventilation, fever, pain.
Q3ABG: pH 7.30, PaCO2 38, HCO3 18. What is the interpretation?
Metabolic Acidosis, uncompensated. pH <7.35 = acidosis. CO2 normal = not respiratory. HCO3 <22 and moving equal to pH = metabolic acidosis. Think: DKA, renal failure, diarrhea, lactic acidosis.
Q4ABG: pH 7.48, PaCO2 48, HCO3 34. What is the interpretation?
Metabolic Alkalosis, partially compensated. pH >7.45 = alkalosis. HCO3 >26 and moves equal to pH = metabolic alkalosis. CO2 is also elevated (lungs retaining CO2 to buffer) = compensation is occurring. pH is still abnormal = partial compensation.
Q5ABG: pH 7.38, PaCO2 50, HCO3 30. What is the interpretation?
Respiratory Acidosis, fully compensated. pH is NORMAL (7.38) but within the acidic side of normal. CO2 elevated = respiratory problem. HCO3 elevated = kidneys have compensated by retaining base. Both values abnormal + pH normalized = fully compensated respiratory acidosis. This is chronic COPD.
Q6A COPD patient is short of breath. SpO2 is 85%. What is the appropriate O2 delivery?
1–2L via nasal cannula — titrate to SpO2 88–92%. COPD patients rely on hypoxic drive. High-flow O2 eliminates the respiratory stimulus → hypoventilation → worsening CO2 retention → respiratory failure. Target the low-normal range, not 100%.
Q7A patient with Type 1 DM presents with deep, rapid breathing and fruity breath. What do you expect the ABG to show?
Metabolic Acidosis (DKA) with respiratory compensation. pH <7.35, HCO3 low (acid accumulation from ketones), CO2 low (Kussmaul respirations blowing off CO2). The fruity breath = ketones on breath. Check glucose (likely >300), ketones, and BMP. Start insulin drip and fluids.
Q8A patient on NG suction for 3 days develops hand cramping and confusion. What acid-base disorder is likely?
Metabolic Alkalosis. NG suction removes gastric HCl (acid) → H+ lost → base excess → pH rises. Symptoms: tetany/cramping (hypocalcemia from alkalosis shifts Ca++ to bound form), confusion. Treat: replace K+ and Cl-, stop/reduce suctioning, antiemetics.
