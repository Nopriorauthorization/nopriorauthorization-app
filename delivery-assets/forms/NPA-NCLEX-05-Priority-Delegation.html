Section 01

Priority Framework: ABC + Maslow

The NCLEX loves "who do you see first" and "what do you do first" questions. The answer almost always follows this two-layer framework: ABCs first, then Maslow's hierarchy for everything else.

🚨
ABC Always Wins
Airway → Breathing → Circulation. No matter what else is happening — a patient who can't breathe or has no pulse is ALWAYS your first priority. Every other concern waits.
📊 Maslow's Hierarchy — Clinical Priority Order
LevelCategoryClinical ExamplesPriority
1PhysiologicalOxygenation, circulation, pain, nutrition, fluid balanceHighest — always first
2SafetyFall prevention, infection control, medication safetyHigh — after physiology
3Love/BelongingFamily support, therapeutic relationshipModerate
4EsteemIndependence, dignity, body imageLower
5Self-ActualizationPersonal growth, reaching full potentialLowest
💡
Physiological Always Beats Psychosocial on NCLEX
A patient crying in distress AND having chest pain → assess the chest pain first. A patient anxious about surgery AND running a fever → address the fever first. Every time.

Section 02

Who to See First?

When you have multiple patients or tasks competing, these are the red-flag findings that signal you go there first — every time.

SEE FIRSTNew-onset chest pain with diaphoresis — possible MI. Airway and circulation emergency. EKG, O2, IV access, aspirin, notify provider.
SEE FIRSTRR <10 or >28 with falling SpO2 — airway/breathing emergency. Open airway, position, O2, call rapid response.
SEE FIRSTSBP <90 with altered mental status — shock. Assess perfusion, IV access, notify provider immediately.
SEE FIRSTSudden change in level of consciousness — hypoglycemia, stroke, sepsis, or overdose until proven otherwise. Check glucose, assess neuro, call provider.
SEE FIRSTPatient just returned from OR (first 30–60 min) — highest instability risk. Always assess before other stable patients.
SEE FIRST"Worst headache of my life" — sudden, severe onset — subarachnoid hemorrhage until proven otherwise. Immediate CT and provider notification.
SEE LASTStable patients requesting comfort measures — sleep aids, chronic pain rated 3/10, scheduled medications, routine dressing changes on stable wounds.
SEE LASTPatient education and discharge planning — important, never urgent. Always last when acute physiological needs exist.

Section 03

Scope of Practice: RN vs LPN vs CNA

The key principle: complexity, instability, and judgment belong to the RN. Stable, routine, predictable tasks can be appropriately delegated.

RN — Registered Nurse
  • Complete initial nursing assessment (head-to-toe)
  • Nursing diagnosis and care plan development and evaluation
  • Patient and family teaching — initiation and evaluation of learning
  • IV push medications and all blood product administration and monitoring
  • Central line care and dressing changes
  • Complex wound care requiring clinical judgment
  • All unstable patients — any acute change in condition
  • Discharge planning and coordination of care
  • Any task requiring nursing assessment or clinical judgment
LPN / LVN — Licensed Practical/Vocational Nurse
  • Ongoing data collection (not the initial full nursing assessment)
  • Routine oral, subcutaneous, and IM medications
  • Routine wound care — stable, predictable, expected outcomes
  • Urinary catheter insertion
  • Gastrostomy tube feedings (after RN confirms placement)
  • IV fluid maintenance — after RN initiates and assesses
  • Reinforcing previously taught patient education (not initiating)
  • Care of stable, chronic patients with predictable outcomes
CNA / UAP — Nursing Assistant
  • Routine vital signs on stable patients (scheduled)
  • Bathing, grooming, oral care, hair care
  • Ambulation and repositioning of stable patients
  • Feeding patients without swallowing difficulties
  • Intake and output measurement and documentation
  • Urine specimen collection (cooperative patients)
  • Applying sequential compression devices (SCDs)
  • Weighing patients, linen changes
🚫
NEVER Delegate to a CNA
Assessment or evaluation of any kind · Any medication administration · Patient or family teaching · Invasive procedures · Unstable patients or any patient with changing condition · Blood transfusion care · Central line or IV medication care

Section 04

5 Rights of Delegation

Delegation is not just assigning a task — it is a formal nursing process. The RN always retains accountability for the patient outcome, regardless of who performs the task.

Right 1Right Task: Is this task appropriate to delegate? Does it require nursing judgment? Routine, stable, predictable tasks with expected outcomes can be delegated.
Right 2Right Circumstance: Is the patient stable? Is the environment safe and appropriate for delegation? An unstable patient changes the circumstance — what was delegatable 20 minutes ago may not be now.
Right 3Right Person: Does this specific individual have the training, competency, experience, and licensure for this specific task? Same title does not mean same competency.
Right 4Right Direction / Communication: Are instructions clear, specific, and understood? Include: what to do, what to observe, what and when to report back, and any patient-specific concerns.
Right 5Right Supervision / Evaluation: Will the RN follow up, evaluate the outcome, and provide feedback? Delegation is not hand-off and forget — supervision is ongoing.
⚖️
Delegation ≠ Loss of Accountability
When an RN delegates a task, the RN remains responsible for the patient outcome. If a CNA takes a vital sign incorrectly and the RN does not follow up, the RN is accountable. Always verify delegated outcomes.

Section 05

SBAR Communication

SBAR is the standard structured format for clinical communication — provider calls, handoffs, rapid response escalation. Know what belongs in each component.

📞 SBAR Framework with Clinical Example
S — Situation
What is happening right now? Brief and specific.
"I'm calling about Mr. Ramirez in room 418. He has new onset chest pain rated 9/10, started 20 minutes ago."
B — Background
Relevant clinical history and context.
"He is a 64-year-old with CAD and hypertension, admitted yesterday for CHF exacerbation. He was stable on telemetry this morning."
A — Assessment
Your clinical interpretation — what you think is happening.
"BP is 94/58, HR 120, SpO2 88% on 2L. He is diaphoretic. Monitor shows ST elevation in V2–V4. I am concerned about an acute MI."
R — Recommendation
What you need or what you recommend.
"I need you here now. Should I increase O2 to NRB mask, get a 12-lead EKG, draw cardiac enzymes, and call the cath lab?"

Section 06

NGN: Clinical Judgment Model

The Next Generation NCLEX tests clinical judgment using a 6-step model. Every NGN item — bow-tie questions, matrix grids, extended drag-and-drop — is built around this framework.

Step 1Recognize Cues: What data is relevant? From the scenario, identify what is abnormal, changing, or significant. Filter out what doesn't matter.
Step 2Analyze Cues: What do the cues mean together? Connect the findings — what systems are involved? Think pathophysiology and clinical picture.
Step 3Prioritize Hypotheses: What is the most likely problem? What is most urgent? Rank by likelihood and immediacy of threat to the patient.
Step 4Generate Solutions: What nursing actions will address the priority? Include both independent nursing actions and collaborative (provider-ordered) interventions.
Step 5Take Action: Implement the highest-priority intervention first. NCLEX frequently tests the correct sequence of actions — order matters.
Step 6Evaluate Outcomes: Did it work? What findings show improvement or deterioration? When do you escalate? Ongoing assessment drives everything that follows.

Section 07

High-Yield NCLEX Questions

Q1You have four patients. Who do you see first? (A) Chronic back pain rated 5/10 requesting pain meds. (B) Post-op patient returned 30 minutes ago, now restless with HR 118. (C) COPD patient asking about discharge meds. (D) Diabetic patient requesting breakfast.
B — post-op patient with tachycardia and restlessness. New-onset tachycardia in a recently returned surgical patient = possible hemorrhage or hemodynamic compromise. This is an acute physiological change. All others are stable and can wait.
Q2Can the RN delegate routine vital signs to a CNA for a stable post-op day 2 patient?
Yes — appropriate delegation. The patient is stable, the task is routine, and the CNA is trained for VS. The RN must receive, evaluate, and act on the results. The assessment of those results is never delegated.
Q3An LPN asks to perform initial discharge teaching for a newly diagnosed diabetic. What should the RN do?
Decline and perform the teaching personally. Initiating patient and family education is an RN-only function. It requires assessment of learning readiness and individualized content. The LPN may reinforce previously taught content, not initiate or lead a new teaching session.
Q4A patient is crying and rates pain 8/10. They also have a new temperature of 102.8°F. What does the nurse address first?
The fever. 102.8°F is an acute physiological concern — possible infection, post-op complication. Pain is important and will be addressed, but physiological problems take priority over pain and psychological distress on the NCLEX.
Q5A CNA reports that a patient's BP is now 80/50 and they seem confused. What is the nurse's first action?
Go to the patient immediately and assess. Hypotension with altered mental status = possible shock. The RN must personally assess — this cannot be managed through the CNA. Get there, check airway and circulation, call provider and/or rapid response.
Q6An overwhelmed RN asks a CNA to give a patient's scheduled oral medications. What should the CNA do?
Decline — medication administration is outside CNA scope. No workload justifies practicing outside of licensure. The CNA should tell the RN they are not permitted to administer medications and suggest notifying the charge nurse about unsafe staffing.
Q7Using SBAR, the nurse calls a provider about a deteriorating patient. What belongs in the Assessment component?
The nurse's clinical interpretation — not just the numbers. Assessment is where you say: "I believe this patient may be in early septic shock" or "I am concerned about a pulmonary embolism." Vitals belong in Situation. The meaning of those vitals — your clinical judgment — belongs in Assessment.
Q8Which task can the RN safely delegate to the LPN? (A) Initial intake assessment on new admit. (B) IV push pain medication. (C) Routine oral medications for a stable patient. (D) Discharge teaching for a new ostomy patient.
C — routine oral medications for a stable patient. This is within LPN scope, the patient is stable, and the outcome is predictable. A = RN only (initial assessment). B = RN only (IV push). D = RN only (initiating new teaching). LPN can give the meds; RN evaluates response.
