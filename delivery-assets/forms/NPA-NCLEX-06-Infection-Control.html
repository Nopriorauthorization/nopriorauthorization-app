Section 01

Standard Precautions

Standard precautions are used with EVERY patient, EVERY time — regardless of diagnosis, isolation status, or appearance of cleanliness. They are the foundation of infection prevention.

AlwaysHand hygiene: Before and after every patient contact. Before and after glove use. After contact with bodily fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or contaminated items. This is the single most effective infection prevention measure.
AlwaysGloves: When contact with blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or contaminated items is anticipated. Change gloves between tasks on the same patient if moving from a contaminated to a clean body site.
AlwaysGown: When clothing or arms may contact blood, body fluids, or contaminated surfaces. Especially during procedures with splash or spray risk.
AlwaysMask and eye protection / face shield: When splash or spray of blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions is anticipated. Protects mucous membranes of eyes, nose, mouth.
AlwaysSafe needle handling: Never recap needles with two hands. Use safety-engineered devices. Dispose immediately in sharps container after use. Never pass uncapped needles hand-to-hand.
AlwaysSafe injection practices: One needle, one syringe, one patient, one time. Never reuse syringes or needles. Never use multi-dose vials for more than one patient without new needle and syringe.
AlwaysRespiratory hygiene / cough etiquette: Teach all patients and visitors to cover coughs and sneezes, perform hand hygiene, and wear a mask when symptomatic with respiratory illness.

Section 02

Transmission-Based Isolation Types

Transmission-based precautions are added ON TOP of standard precautions based on how a specific pathogen spreads. Know the diseases, the PPE required, and the room type for each.

Contact Precautions
PPE Required: Gown + Gloves (upon room entry)
Room: Private room preferred; cohort if unavailable. Keep door closed. Dedicated equipment (stethoscope, BP cuff — stays in room).

Diseases: MRSA, VRE, C. diff (C. diff also requires soap and water — see Section 05), RSV, wound infections, scabies, lice, impetigo, norovirus, rotavirus, any contact-transmissible multi-drug-resistant organism (MDRO).

Transport: Limit patient transport to essential only. Notify receiving department. Cover wounds and ensure patient performs hand hygiene before leaving room.
Droplet Precautions
PPE Required: Surgical Mask (within 3 feet / upon room entry)
Room: Private room preferred. Door may remain open (droplets don't travel far — generally <3–6 feet). Special air handling NOT required.

Diseases: Influenza (flu), meningitis (bacterial — Neisseria meningitidis), pertussis (whooping cough), mumps, rubella, Group A strep pharyngitis, adenovirus, rhinovirus.

Memory tip: MMMR Flu — Mumps, Meningitis, Rubella, and Flu are classic droplet pathogens.

Transport: Patient wears surgical mask during transport.
Airborne Precautions
PPE Required: N95 Respirator (fitted) + Negative Pressure Room
Room: Negative pressure room (air flows in, not out). Door must remain CLOSED at all times. 6–12 air exchanges per hour. Only fit-tested staff with N95 respirators enter.

Diseases: MTV — Measles (rubeola), Tuberculosis (TB), Varicella (chickenpox) / disseminated Herpes Zoster (shingles). Also: SARS, MERS, monkeypox (some guidelines).

Memory tip: MTV goes Airborne — Measles, TB, Varicella.

Transport: Patient wears N95 (or surgical mask if N95 unavailable). Notify receiving area. Minimize time outside room.
Protective / Reverse Isolation
PPE: Varies by protocol — protects the immunocompromised patient FROM staff and environment
Used for: Severely immunocompromised patients — bone marrow transplant, ANC <500/μL, high-dose chemotherapy.

Goal is reversed: Instead of protecting staff from the patient, you are protecting the patient from organisms that staff and visitors might carry in.

Precautions: Staff and visitors wear gown, gloves, mask before entering. No fresh flowers or live plants (harbor mold). No fresh fruits/vegetables. HEPA filtration or positive pressure rooms used in some facilities.

Section 03

PPE Donning & Doffing Order

The order of putting on (donning) and taking off (doffing) PPE is tested directly on the NCLEX. The goal: prevent contamination of yourself, your clothing, or clean surfaces during removal.

✅ DONNING Order (Putting On)
  1. Gown — tie at waist and neck
  2. Mask or Respirator — fit check N95 if used
  3. Goggles or Face Shield
  4. Gloves — extend over gown cuffs
Memory: G-M-G-G
Gown → Mask → Goggles → Gloves
⚠️ DOFFING Order (Taking Off)
  1. Gloves — most contaminated, remove first
  2. Goggles / Face Shield — handle by straps only
  3. Gown — unfasten, peel away from body, roll inward
  4. Mask / Respirator — handle by straps, remove last (least contaminated)
  5. Hand hygiene immediately after every step
Memory: Remove most contaminated first.
Gloves → Goggles → Gown → Mask
🚨
Doffing = Highest Contamination Risk
Most healthcare-associated transmission occurs during PPE removal, not during patient care. Perform hand hygiene between EACH step of doffing. Never touch your face, hair, or clothing with contaminated gloves. Treat the outside of all PPE as contaminated.

Section 04

Chain of Infection

Infection requires all 6 links of the chain to remain intact. Break any one link and you prevent transmission. This is the framework behind every infection control intervention.

Link 1Infectious Agent: The pathogen — bacteria, virus, fungus, parasite. Break it by: sterilization, disinfection, antibiotics, antivirals.
Link 2Reservoir: Where the pathogen lives and multiplies — patients, staff, equipment, water, soil. Break it by: treating infected individuals, proper food handling, clean equipment, wound care.
Link 3Portal of Exit: How the pathogen leaves the reservoir — respiratory secretions, wound drainage, feces, blood, urine. Break it by: covering coughs, wound dressings, standard precautions.
Link 4Mode of Transmission: How it travels — contact (direct/indirect), droplet, airborne, vehicle (food/water), vector. Break it by: hand hygiene, PPE, isolation precautions, sterilization of equipment.
Link 5Portal of Entry: How it enters a new host — respiratory tract, GI tract, skin breaks, mucous membranes, urinary tract. Break it by: intact skin care, sterile technique for invasive procedures, PPE for mucous membranes.
Link 6Susceptible Host: A person vulnerable to the pathogen — immunocompromised, elderly, malnourished, post-op, diabetic. Break it by: vaccinations, improving host defenses, nutrition support, treating underlying disease.
💡
Hand Hygiene Breaks the Most Links
Hand hygiene interrupts transmission at multiple points: it removes organisms from the portal of exit, eliminates them as a mode of transmission (contact), and prevents entry through the portal of entry. It is the single most important infection control intervention — tested on every NCLEX.

Section 05

Special Rules: C. diff & Other Exceptions

A handful of pathogens have rules that differ from the general framework. These exceptions are heavily tested because they trip up students who memorize the basics but miss the details.

🧼
C. diff = Soap and Water ONLY — No Alcohol
Clostridium difficile (C. diff) forms spores that are resistant to alcohol-based hand sanitizer. Alcohol does NOT kill C. diff spores. Always use soap and water for hand hygiene when caring for a C. diff patient. This is one of the most commonly tested infection control facts on NCLEX.
🫁
Varicella / Shingles: Both Airborne AND Contact
Varicella (chickenpox) and disseminated herpes zoster (shingles) require BOTH airborne precautions (N95, negative pressure room) AND contact precautions (gown + gloves). This dual-precaution requirement is tested frequently. Localized shingles in an immunocompetent patient = contact precautions only.
😷
TB: Airborne — Patient Wears Surgical Mask for Transport
For TB, the staff wear N95 respirators (fitted, not just a surgical mask). The patient wears a surgical mask during transport outside the room. This is a critical distinction — the N95 protects the nurse, not the surgical mask. A surgical mask on the nurse is NOT sufficient for TB.
💡
Neutropenic Precautions
For patients with ANC <500/μL: no fresh flowers, no live plants (mold risk), no fresh uncooked fruits or vegetables, no ill visitors. Screen visitors for illness. Staff must perform thorough hand hygiene. This is protective (reverse) isolation — you are protecting the patient from the environment.
⚠️
Section 06

High-Yield NCLEX Questions

Q1A patient is admitted with active pulmonary tuberculosis. What type of room and PPE are required?
Negative pressure room (airborne precautions) + N95 respirator for all staff entering. The door must remain closed at all times. The patient wears a surgical mask during any transport outside the room. Standard surgical masks on staff are not sufficient for TB.
Q2A nurse finishes caring for a patient with C. diff. What hand hygiene should be performed?
Soap and water — not alcohol-based hand sanitizer. C. diff spores are resistant to alcohol. Soap and water mechanically removes the spores from hands. This is non-negotiable for C. diff care. Use this rule every time C. diff appears on the NCLEX.
Q3A patient is admitted with MRSA in a wound. What precautions are required?
Contact precautions: private room, gown and gloves upon room entry. Dedicated equipment stays in the room (stethoscope, BP cuff). Limit transport. Notify receiving departments before transport. Document MRSA status prominently in the chart.
Q4The nurse is removing PPE after caring for a patient on contact precautions. In what order should PPE be removed?
Gloves → Goggles/Face Shield → Gown → Mask. Perform hand hygiene between each step. Remove gloves first because they are the most contaminated. Remove the mask last because it is closest to your face and least contaminated by the patient environment.
Q5A patient with chickenpox is admitted. What precautions are required?
Both airborne AND contact precautions. Varicella spreads via airborne particles AND direct contact with lesions. N95 respirator + gown + gloves + negative pressure room. Only immune staff should enter if possible. This dual-precaution requirement is a classic NCLEX trap.
Q6A patient with influenza is admitted. What type of isolation is required?
Droplet precautions: surgical mask for staff within 3 feet, private room preferred, door may remain open. Influenza spreads via large respiratory droplets — not airborne particles. A surgical mask (not N95) is appropriate. N95 is NOT required for standard influenza.
Q7Which action most effectively breaks the chain of infection at the "mode of transmission" link?
Hand hygiene. Handwashing removes pathogens from hands before they can be transferred to a new host. This directly breaks the mode of transmission — specifically contact transmission, which is the most common route of healthcare-associated infection.
Q8A bone marrow transplant patient has an ANC of 300/μL. A family member arrives with a runny nose and wants to visit. What should the nurse do?
Ask the visitor not to enter the patient's room. ANC <500 = severe neutropenia = protective precautions. Ill visitors are a direct infection risk. Explain the situation kindly to the family member and facilitate alternative communication (video call, phone). The patient's safety comes first.
