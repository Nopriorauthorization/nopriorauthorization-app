Section 01

Stages of Labor

Labor has four stages and each is tested on the NCLEX. Know the cervical dilation, duration, behaviors, and key nursing priorities for each phase.

📊 Labor Stages Reference
Stage/PhaseDilationDurationCharacteristicsNursing Priority
Stage 1 — Latent0–6 cmHours (longer in primip)Mild, irregular contractions; talkative, excited or anxiousAssess baseline FHR, cervical exam, comfort measures
Stage 1 — Active6–10 cmShorter, more intenseModerate–strong contractions q3–5 min; focused, less talkativeContinuous FHR monitoring, pain management, positioning
Stage 1 — Transition8–10 cm30 min–1 hrStrong, frequent contractions q2–3 min; most intense phase; may be irrational/exhaustedIntensive support, encourage breathing, do not leave patient
Stage 2 — Pushing10 cm (complete)Minutes to 2+ hoursUrge to push, rectal pressure, fetal crowningCoach pushing, monitor FHR continuously, prepare for delivery
Stage 3 — PlacentaPost-delivery5–30 minPlacental separation signs: gush of blood, cord lengthening, uterus firms and risesWatch for PPH, fundal massage, Pitocin after delivery
Stage 4 — RecoveryPost-placenta1–4 hoursUterus contracts, lochia begins, VS stabilizeQ15 min VS and fundal assessments for first hour
💡
Transition = Most Intense Phase
Transition (8–10 cm) is the hardest part of labor for most patients. Contractions peak at 60–90 seconds every 2–3 minutes with almost no rest between. Patients may be shaking, vomiting, crying, or saying they cannot continue. This is normal — it means delivery is very close. The nurse's role: stay present, coach breathing, do not leave.

Section 02

Fetal Heart Rate Patterns

FHR monitoring is a core OB nursing skill. The NCLEX tests pattern identification and — most importantly — what to DO when you see each pattern.

📊 FHR Pattern Reference
PatternDescriptionCauseNurse Action
Normal Baseline110–160 bpm, moderate variabilityNormal fetal autonomic functionContinue monitoring, document
Early DecelerationsGradual ↓, mirror contraction shape, returns to baseline with contraction endHead compression — benignContinue monitoring, reassuring pattern
Variable DecelerationsAbrupt ↓, V or U shape, variable timing relative to contractionCord compressionReposition (side-lying, knee-chest), O2 by mask, IV fluid bolus, notify provider
Late DecelerationsGradual ↓, begins AFTER contraction peaks, returns after contraction endsUteroplacental insufficiency — EMERGENCYReposition (L lateral), stop Pitocin, O2 10L NRB, IV fluid bolus, notify provider STAT, prepare for C-section
Prolonged DecelerationFHR <110 for >2 min but <10 minCord prolapse, abruption, maternal hypotensionIdentify cause, reposition, O2, call provider, prepare for emergency delivery
🚨
Late Decelerations = EMERGENCY — Act Immediately
Late decelerations signal that the placenta is not delivering enough oxygen to the fetus. Every second counts. The sequence: (1) Reposition to left lateral, (2) Stop Pitocin, (3) O2 10L via NRB mask, (4) IV fluid bolus, (5) Notify provider immediately, (6) Prepare for possible emergency C-section. Do all of this simultaneously, not sequentially.
⚠️
Variable Decelerations — First Action: Reposition
Variable decelerations are caused by cord compression — the cord is being squeezed during contractions. The first nursing action is always to reposition the patient (left lateral, right lateral, or knee-chest position) to relieve cord pressure. Then O2 and fluids.

Section 03

OB Emergencies

These are the high-stakes obstetric emergencies the NCLEX tests heavily. Know the defining characteristic, the immediate nursing action, and why.

PPHPostpartum Hemorrhage: Blood loss >500 mL vaginal, >1000 mL C-section. Most common cause = uterine atony (soft, "boggy" uterus). Action: Fundal massage, empty bladder, Pitocin (oxytocin) IV, notify provider, large-bore IV access, type and crossmatch, O2.
PreeclampsiaPreeclampsia: BP ≥140/90 on two occasions + proteinuria after 20 weeks. Symptoms: HA, visual changes, RUQ pain, edema, hyperreflexia. Action: Mag sulfate (seizure prophylaxis), antihypertensives (labetalol, hydralazine), continuous monitoring, keep calcium gluconate at bedside (Mag antidote).
EclampsiaEclampsia = preeclampsia + seizure. During seizure: Do NOT restrain, protect from injury, position on side, O2, time seizure. After: Mag sulfate IV bolus + maintenance, assess fetal status, prepare for delivery. Mag antidote = calcium gluconate.
AbruptionAbruptio Placentae (placental abruption): Premature separation of the placenta. Classic: PAINFUL, dark red bleeding, rigid/board-like abdomen, FHR changes. Emergency delivery likely. Large-bore IV, blood products, continuous FHR monitoring.
PreviaPlacenta Previa: Placenta covers the cervical os. Classic: PAINLESS, bright red vaginal bleeding, no abdominal pain, soft uterus. NO vaginal exams (can cause hemorrhage). Bed rest, C-section delivery, monitor FHR.
Cord ProlapseUmbilical Cord Prolapse: Cord precedes the presenting part through the cervix — being compressed = fetal hypoxia. First action: relieve cord pressure. Place patient in knee-chest or Trendelenburg position. Do NOT push cord back in — keep moist and hold cord off presenting part. Call code/provider. Emergency C-section.
Shoulder DystociaShoulder Dystocia: Head delivers but anterior shoulder is impacted behind the pubic symphysis. McRoberts maneuver first (hyperflexing thighs against abdomen) + suprapubic pressure (not fundal — pushes shoulder deeper). Call for help immediately, prepare for emergency intervention.
💡
Abruption vs. Previa — The Key Distinction
Abruption = PAIN + dark red bleeding + rigid abdomen. "ABruptio = ABdominal pain."
Previa = NO pain + bright red bleeding + soft uterus. Both are emergencies, but the distinction drives the assessment and interventions. Previa = no vaginal exam ever.

Section 04

APGAR Score

The APGAR score is assessed at 1 minute and 5 minutes after birth. It evaluates 5 signs, each scored 0–2. Maximum score = 10. The NCLEX tests individual components and score interpretation.

📊 APGAR Scoring System
Component012
A — Appearance (color)Blue/pale all overPink body, blue extremities (acrocyanosis)Pink all over
P — Pulse (heart rate)Absent<100 bpm≥100 bpm
G — Grimace (reflex)No responseGrimaceCry, cough, sneeze
A — Activity (muscle tone)Limp, no movementSome flexionActive motion, flexed
R — RespirationsAbsentWeak, irregularStrong cry
🔢 Score Interpretation
Score RangeInterpretationNursing Action
7–10Normal — reassuringRoutine newborn care, skin-to-skin, breastfeeding support
4–6Moderately depressedStimulation, supplemental O2, reassess at 5 minutes
0–3Severely depressedImmediate resuscitation — warm, dry, stimulate, O2, positive pressure ventilation, call NICU
💡
APGAR Is Not a Resuscitation Guide
Resuscitation does not wait for the 1-minute APGAR. If the baby is not breathing or HR is <100, begin interventions immediately. The APGAR score is a standardized assessment tool completed after initial stabilization — it documents, it doesn't decide when to act.

Section 05

Postpartum Assessment: BUBBLE-HE

BUBBLE-HE is the systematic postpartum head-to-toe assessment framework. Each letter represents a component assessed during the postpartum period. The NCLEX tests expected vs abnormal findings.

BBreasts: Firm, non-tender initially → tender as milk comes in (~day 2–3). Colostrum first 2–3 days. Assess for engorgement, cracked nipples, mastitis (hard, warm, red area + fever). Encourage frequent feeding.
UUterus (Fundus): Firm, midline, at umbilicus immediately postpartum. Descends 1 cm/day. By day 10: below symphysis pubis. Soft (boggy) fundus = uterine atony → massage until firm and call provider. Deviated = full bladder → empty bladder first.
BBladder: Voiding within 6–8 hours of delivery. Assess for distension, urgency, burning. Full bladder displaces uterus and prevents it from contracting → PPH risk. Catheterize if unable to void.
BBowel: Bowel sounds return; first BM typically by day 2–3. Assess for constipation, hemorrhoids, pain with defecation. Encourage fluids, fiber, ambulation, stool softeners as ordered. Hemorrhoids very common postpartum.
LLochia: Postpartum vaginal discharge. Rubra (red) days 1–3. Serosa (pink/brown) days 4–10. Alba (yellow/white) days 10–14 up to 6 weeks. Abnormal: heavy bleeding (saturating >1 pad/hr), foul odor (infection), return to red after serosa (retained placenta).
EEpisiotomy / Perineum: Assess with REEDA: Redness, Edema, Ecchymosis, Discharge, Approximation. Mild edema and tenderness = normal. Ice first 24 hrs, then sitz baths. Report: increasing pain, purulent discharge, wound separation.
HHoman's Sign / DVT: Assess calves for pain, redness, warmth, swelling. Note: Homan's sign (pain with dorsiflexion) is no longer considered reliable for DVT diagnosis — use clinical judgment + duplex ultrasound. Encourage early ambulation to prevent DVT.
EEmotions / Bonding: "Baby blues" (tearfulness, mood swings) days 1–5 = normal hormonal adjustment. Postpartum depression: persistent sadness, difficulty bonding, hopelessness — refer to provider. Postpartum psychosis: hallucinations, delusions, confusion — psychiatric emergency, do not leave patient alone.

Section 06

Pitocin (Oxytocin) Nursing Care

Pitocin is used to induce or augment labor. It is a high-alert medication — the nurse must assess for both under-effect (ineffective labor) and over-effect (uterine hyperstimulation).

BeforeAssess: FHR baseline (must be within normal limits), contraction pattern (frequency, duration, intensity), cervical status, maternal VS. Do NOT start Pitocin if FHR is non-reassuring or uterus is already hyperstimulated.
DuringMonitor continuously: FHR and contraction pattern on external monitor. Assess contractions: normal = lasting <90 seconds, occurring no more than q2 min with adequate rest between. Maternal VS every 30–60 min. Urine output (Pitocin has antidiuretic properties — monitor for fluid retention).
STOPHyperstimulation = stop Pitocin immediately: Contractions longer than 90 seconds, occurring more than q2 minutes, or uterus is not relaxing between contractions. Signs: late decelerations, fetal bradycardia, prolonged deceleration. Also stop for: maternal SBP >160, FHR non-reassuring pattern.
ActionWhen Pitocin is stopped for hyperstimulation: Reposition to left lateral, O2 10L via NRB, IV fluid bolus, notify provider STAT. Terbutaline (tocolytic) may be ordered to relax the uterus. Prepare for possible emergency delivery.
⚠️
Mag Sulfate in OB — Critical Monitoring
Magnesium sulfate is given for seizure prophylaxis in preeclampsia and as a tocolytic for preterm labor. Monitor: RR (hold if <12), DTRs (loss of DTRs = first sign of toxicity), urine output (<30 mL/hr = hold), LOC. Antidote at bedside always: Calcium gluconate 1g IV. Mag is also a NCLEX-classic high-alert medication.
⚠️
Section 07

High-Yield NCLEX Questions

Q1The FHR monitor shows late decelerations. What is the nurse's first action?
Reposition to left lateral position and stop Pitocin if infusing. Late decelerations = uteroplacental insufficiency. The full response: reposition (left lateral), stop Pitocin, O2 10L NRB mask, IV fluid bolus, notify provider STAT, prepare for potential emergency C-section. All simultaneously.
Q2A patient has painless bright red vaginal bleeding at 32 weeks. What is the priority nursing action?
Do NOT perform a vaginal exam — suspect placenta previa. Placenta previa = painless bleeding + low-lying placenta covering the cervix. A vaginal exam can cause catastrophic hemorrhage. Call provider, continuous FHR monitoring, IV access, prepare for possible C-section. Bed rest.
Q3A laboring patient begins seizing. What is the nurse's priority?
Protect from injury, do not restrain, position on side, O2, time the seizure. This is eclampsia. After seizure ends: Mag sulfate bolus + maintenance, assess fetal status, notify provider for emergency delivery planning. Keep calcium gluconate at bedside (Mag antidote).
Q4A newborn has APGAR scores of 1, 2, 0, 1, 0 at 1 minute. What is the score and action?
Score = 4 — moderate depression. Begin resuscitation. Warm, dry, stimulate, supplemental O2. If no improvement: positive pressure ventilation. Reassess at 5 minutes. Score 4–6 = moderately depressed. Score 0–3 = severely depressed and requires immediate full resuscitation including PPV and possibly chest compressions.
Q5A postpartum patient's uterus is deviated to the right and she hasn't voided. What should the nurse do first?
Assist the patient to void. A deviated uterus = full bladder displacing the uterus. A full bladder prevents uterine contraction → increases PPH risk. Assist to bathroom or insert catheter if unable to void. After bladder is empty, reassess the fundus — it should return to midline and firm up.
Q6A nurse sees the umbilical cord prolapsing through the cervix. What is the immediate action?
Relieve cord compression immediately — place patient in knee-chest or Trendelenburg position. Use a gloved hand to hold the presenting part off the cord inside the vagina. Do NOT push the cord back in. Keep the cord moist. Call for emergency C-section. This is a 1-minute emergency.
Q7A patient on Mag sulfate has a RR of 10 and absent DTRs. What should the nurse do?
Stop the Mag sulfate immediately and give calcium gluconate IV. Absent DTRs = first sign of Mag toxicity. RR <12 confirms toxicity. Calcium gluconate 1g (10 mL of 10% solution) is the antidote — given slowly IV over 3 minutes. Notify provider, O2, monitor continuously.
Q8A postpartum patient is saturating more than 1 pad per hour. What is the priority?
Assess the uterus — palpate the fundus. Heavy lochia saturating >1 pad/hour = postpartum hemorrhage. Check fundal height and tone. If soft/boggy → fundal massage, empty bladder, Pitocin per order, notify provider. If firm → assess for cervical/vaginal lacerations.
