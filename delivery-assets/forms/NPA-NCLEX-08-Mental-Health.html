Section 01

Therapeutic Communication Techniques

Therapeutic communication is the cornerstone of the nurse-patient relationship. The NCLEX tests this heavily — often presenting a patient statement and asking which nurse response is most therapeutic.

Open-Ended QuestionsEncourage the patient to talk freely. "Tell me more about what you've been feeling." "What has this experience been like for you?" These cannot be answered with yes/no and invite elaboration. Always preferred over closed questions in therapeutic communication.
ReflectionMirror the patient's feelings or words back to them. Patient: "I'm so scared." Nurse: "You're feeling scared." This validates the emotion and encourages the patient to explore it further. Does not interpret or project meaning.
Restating / ParaphrasingRepeat the main idea in slightly different words. Patient: "I can't sleep, I keep thinking about my diagnosis." Nurse: "It sounds like your diagnosis has been on your mind at night." Confirms understanding and shows active listening.
ClarificationAsk for more information when something is unclear. "I'm not sure I understand — can you tell me more about that?" Prevents assumptions and ensures accurate understanding. Appropriate when a patient's message is ambiguous.
FocusingDirect attention to a specific topic. "Let's focus on what happened this morning." Useful when a patient is scattered or jumping between topics. Not controlling — guides toward what matters most clinically.
SilencePurposeful, comfortable silence allows the patient to think and speak at their own pace. Resist the urge to fill every silence — silence is a therapeutic tool, not awkwardness to fix. Communicate presence through eye contact and open body language.
Broad OpeningsLet the patient lead the direction of the conversation. "Where would you like to begin?" "What would you like to talk about today?" Conveys respect for patient autonomy and encourages them to identify what matters most to them.
SummarizingReview key points at the end of an interaction. "So today we talked about your feelings about the diagnosis and your concerns about telling your family. Does that sound right?" Reinforces learning and ensures nothing important was missed.

Section 02

Non-Therapeutic Communication — What to AVOID

The NCLEX presents non-therapeutic responses as wrong answer choices — but students choose them because they sound kind or reassuring. Know why each one fails.

❌ Non-Therapeutic Techniques and Why They Fail
TechniqueExampleWhy It's Non-Therapeutic
False Reassurance"Everything will be fine. Don't worry."Dismisses the patient's legitimate concern; you cannot guarantee outcomes
Giving Advice"You should talk to your family about this."Undermines patient autonomy; implies you know better; not the nurse's role
Changing the Subject"Let's not focus on that — how did you sleep?"Avoids the patient's concern; signals it's unwelcome or unimportant
Offering Personal Opinion"If I were you, I would..."Projects nurse values; shifts focus to the nurse; undermines patient decision-making
Using "Why" Questions"Why did you do that?"Sounds accusatory or judgmental; puts patient on the defensive
Agreeing or Disagreeing"You're absolutely right" or "That's wrong."Passing judgment — good or bad — discourages further sharing; not therapeutic neutrality
Belittling Feelings"Everyone goes through this, it's not that bad."Invalidates the patient's experience; minimizes distress
Clichés"Every cloud has a silver lining."Dismissive platitudes that offer no real support or understanding
Closed-Ended Questions"Are you feeling sad?"Limits to yes/no; shuts down conversation; leads the patient to a specific answer
💡
NCLEX Tip: False Reassurance is the Most Common Wrong Answer
When a patient expresses fear, sadness, or uncertainty, the NCLEX almost always includes "Everything will be okay" or "Don't worry" as an option — and almost always it is WRONG. The correct answer will acknowledge the feeling and invite further exploration, not dismiss it.

Section 03

Suicide Risk & Safety

Suicide assessment and safety planning are heavily tested on the NCLEX. Know the risk factors, assessment approach, and the nursing priorities for each level of risk.

💬
Ask Directly — It Does Not Increase Risk
Research consistently shows that asking directly about suicide does NOT increase the risk of suicide. In fact, asking shows the patient you are taking them seriously. On the NCLEX, if a patient expresses hopelessness or suicidal ideation, the nurse's first action is to ask directly: "Are you thinking about harming or killing yourself?"
⚠️ Suicide Risk Assessment
Risk FactorHigher Risk Indicators
PlanHas a specific, detailed plan (more dangerous than vague ideation)
MeansHas access to the planned method (gun, medications, etc.)
TimelineHas a specific time in mind for the attempt
HistoryPrevious suicide attempts (strongest predictor of future attempt)
Hopelessness"Nothing will ever get better" — more predictive than depression alone
IsolationNo social support, recently lost relationships, withdrawn
Giving things awaySuddenly giving away prized possessions — may signal farewell behavior
Sudden calmPatient who was previously distressed suddenly appears at peace — may mean decision has been made
Priority 1Remove means (environmental safety): Remove sharps, medications, belts, cords, ligature risks from the environment. Search belongings and room. This is the most immediate safety action.
Priority 2One-to-one observation (1:1): For high-risk patients, continuous 1:1 monitoring — the nurse or assigned staff member stays within arm's reach at all times. Do not leave the patient alone. Not even for a moment.
Priority 3Therapeutic relationship — do not bargain or promise confidentiality: Never say "I won't tell anyone." You are legally and ethically obligated to report. You CAN say "I will keep you safe" and "I care about what happens to you."
Priority 4Safety contract vs. safety planning: Current evidence does not support traditional "no-harm contracts" — they are not reliable. A collaborative safety plan that identifies triggers, coping skills, support people, and crisis contacts is preferred.

Section 04

Psychiatric Disorders — Key Nursing Points

Know the defining symptoms, the NCLEX-tested nursing priority, and the key therapeutic and non-therapeutic approach for each major psychiatric disorder.

SchizophreniaHallucinations and delusions: Do NOT argue with, reinforce, or play along with delusions or hallucinations. Acknowledge the patient's feelings ("I can see you are frightened") without validating the false belief. Safety: assess for command hallucinations (voices telling them to harm themselves or others). Antipsychotics are first-line.
Bipolar — ManiaSafety during mania: High energy, grandiosity, poor judgment, impulsivity, decreased sleep. Priority = safety. Provide quiet environment, reduce stimulation. Manic patients may not eat (too busy) → offer high-calorie foods they can carry and eat on the go. Lithium is gold-standard; monitor level 0.6–1.2 mEq/L.
Major DepressionAssess for suicidal ideation with every depressed patient. SSRIs take 2–4 weeks for full effect — teach this to prevent early discontinuation. Watch for "lifting" of depression (patient may now have energy to act on a plan). Therapeutic activity: structured, achievable tasks to build self-esteem.
Anxiety / PanicDuring a panic attack: Stay calm and present, speak slowly and softly, guide breathing, reduce environmental stimulation. Do not leave the patient. After it passes: debrief, identify triggers, discuss coping strategies. Benzodiazepines for acute anxiety (short-term only — addiction risk).
PTSDTriggers and flashbacks: Create a safe, predictable environment. Approach slowly, explain all procedures before touching. Avoid restraints if at all possible — can worsen trauma response. Respect autonomy and control. SSRIs are first-line pharmacotherapy.
OCDDo not interrupt rituals abruptly — this increases anxiety. Allow adequate time for rituals when safe to do so. Goal of treatment is to reduce frequency and distress, not immediately eliminate behaviors. CBT (Exposure and Response Prevention) + SSRIs are first-line.
Borderline PDSplitting behavior: Patient alternates between idealizing ("You're the only nurse who understands me") and devaluing ("You're terrible") staff. Consistent team approach is essential — set clear, firm, consistent boundaries. Do not deviate from the treatment plan. Validate feelings, but maintain limits.

Psychiatric Medications

Know the class, primary use, key nursing considerations, and major side effects. The NCLEX tests psychiatric medication side effects and what to do about them more than most other pharmacology topics.

Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS) — Know All Four Types
Akathisia: Restlessness, inability to sit still — often mistaken for anxiety.
Dystonia: Sudden, sustained muscle spasms — torticollis, oculogyric crisis (eyes roll upward). Treat with Benztropine IM/IV.
Pseudoparkinsonism: Shuffling gait, tremor, pill-rolling, mask-like face — looks like Parkinson's disease.
Tardive Dyskinesia: Late-onset, repetitive, involuntary movements (lip smacking, tongue thrusting, facial grimacing) — may be irreversible. Stop the offending antipsychotic.
🌡️
Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS) — Life-Threatening Emergency
NMS = fever + muscle rigidity + altered consciousness + autonomic instability (BP swings, tachycardia, diaphoresis). Can occur with any antipsychotic. Action: Stop the antipsychotic immediately, supportive care, dantrolene may be ordered, ICU monitoring. NMS is often confused with serotonin syndrome — key difference: NMS has muscle rigidity + fever; serotonin syndrome has clonus + tremor + diarrhea.

Crisis Intervention Principles

Crisis intervention is a short-term, problem-focused approach to helping a patient regain equilibrium after a precipitating event overwhelms their coping abilities.

Step 1Assess safety and lethality: Is the patient in immediate danger? Suicide risk? Harm to others? This always comes first. Ensure a safe environment before anything else.
Step 2Establish rapport and communication: Introduce yourself, use a calm tone, maintain non-threatening body language. The therapeutic relationship is the foundation of all crisis work.
Step 3Identify the precipitating event: "What happened that brought you here today?" or "What was happening right before you felt this way?" Focus on the event, not the entire history.
Step 4Assess coping and support: What has the patient tried? What has worked before? Who can they call? Identify existing strengths and support systems — these are assets to leverage.
Step 5Generate and evaluate alternatives: Collaboratively brainstorm coping options. What could the patient do differently? What resources are available? Restore a sense of control and hope.
Step 6Develop a plan and follow up: Create a concrete, specific plan. Who will the patient contact if they feel unsafe? What will they do in the next 24 hours? Arrange follow-up care and provide crisis resources.
🏥
Involuntary Hospitalization Criteria
A patient can be held involuntarily when they are: (1) Danger to self (suicidal with plan and intent), (2) Danger to others (homicidal ideation with plan and intent), or (3) Gravely disabled (unable to provide for basic needs due to mental illness). The RN documents these criteria carefully. The physician or designated provider initiates the hold. The RN ensures safety during the process.

Section 07

High-Yield NCLEX Questions

Q1A patient says "I've been thinking that everyone would be better off without me." What is the nurse's best response?
"Are you thinking about harming or killing yourself?" Ask directly. This validates that the nurse heard the concern and does not increase suicide risk. Never redirect ("Let's talk about something happier") or minimize ("I'm sure that's not true"). Direct assessment is always the priority.
Q2A patient with schizophrenia says insects are crawling under her skin. What is the best nurse response?
"I don't see insects, but I can see you are very distressed." Do not argue with or reinforce the hallucination. Acknowledge the feeling (distress is real) without validating the false perception. Assess for safety (are the hallucinations commanding harmful behavior?), stay calm, and administer antipsychotic medication as ordered.
Q3A patient newly prescribed sertraline (Zoloft) says "I've been on it a week and I feel no better. I want to stop taking it." What does the nurse teach?
SSRIs take 2–4 weeks to reach therapeutic effect. Teach the patient this is expected and not to stop abruptly — abrupt discontinuation causes discontinuation syndrome (flu-like symptoms, dizziness, "brain zaps"). Encourage them to continue for at least 4–6 weeks before evaluating effectiveness with their provider.
Q4A patient on haloperidol develops sudden sustained contraction of the neck muscles (torticollis). What is the priority?
Administer Benztropine (Cogentin) IM or IV as ordered — acute dystonia. This is an EPS reaction (dystonia) requiring immediate treatment. It is frightening and painful. Benztropine is the antidote. Reassure the patient, notify the provider, and document. The antipsychotic dose may need to be adjusted.
Q5A patient on lithium reports tremors, vomiting, and confusion. Lithium level is 2.0 mEq/L. What should the nurse do?
Hold lithium, notify provider immediately — lithium toxicity. Level >1.5 mEq/L = toxic. Symptoms: tremor, N/V, diarrhea, ataxia, confusion → seizures, coma if level continues to rise. Treatment is supportive — hydrate with NS (promotes Li excretion), monitor renal function, dialysis if severe. There is no specific antidote.
Q6A patient who was suicidal this morning suddenly appears calm and says she has "made a decision." What does the nurse do?
Increase observation level immediately — this may signal she has decided to act on a plan. Sudden calmness after suicidal ideation is a red flag, not reassurance. Notify provider, assess environmental safety (remove means), initiate 1:1 monitoring, document findings. Do not assume calm = improved.
Q7A patient on an antipsychotic develops a temperature of 104°F, severe muscle rigidity, and diaphoresis. What does the nurse suspect and do?
Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS) — stop the antipsychotic immediately. NMS = fever + rigidity + altered consciousness + autonomic instability. Call provider, supportive care, ICU-level monitoring, dantrolene may be ordered. This is life-threatening. NMS is different from serotonin syndrome (NMS has rigidity; serotonin syndrome has clonus and diarrhea).
Q8A patient on an MAOI eats a pepperoni pizza with wine. Hours later they have a severe headache and BP of 210/128. What happened?
Tyramine-induced hypertensive crisis. MAOIs block monoamine oxidase → tyramine in aged/fermented foods accumulates → massive catecholamine release → severe hypertension. Teach patients to avoid: aged cheeses, wine, beer, cured meats, soy sauce, fermented foods. This is a medical emergency requiring IV antihypertensives.
Q9A patient in crisis says "I need you to promise not to tell anyone what I'm about to tell you." What should the nurse say?
"I can't make that promise, but I can tell you I'll do everything I can to keep you safe." Never promise confidentiality to a psychiatric patient. Nurses are legally and ethically obligated to report threats of harm to self or others. Offering false promises destroys trust and creates legal liability.
Q10Which response is most therapeutic when a patient says "I feel like no one cares about me"?
"It sounds like you're feeling very alone right now." This is reflection — it mirrors the patient's feeling without judgment, interpretation, or false reassurance. Wrong answers typically include: "I care about you!" (false reassurance), "Why do you feel that way?" (why = accusatory), or "You have people who love you" (dismissive).
