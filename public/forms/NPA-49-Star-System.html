Why Your Rating Is Your Revenue
The math behind your stars — and why one bad review feels like the end of the world
From Danielle

The Numbers Every Provider Needs to Know

How reviews affect your bookings

  • 94% of consumers say a negative review has convinced them to avoid a business
  • A one-star increase in rating correlates with a 5–9% increase in revenue
  • Patients trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations from friends
  • The average consumer reads 10 reviews before feeling they can trust a business
  • 53% of patients will not book a provider with less than a 4-star rating
  • Responding to reviews increases the likelihood of a booking by 18%

How Google ranks your business

  • Google uses your rating, review volume, and review recency as local ranking signals
  • A practice with 200 reviews at 4.8 outranks one with 20 reviews at 5.0 — volume matters
  • Review recency matters — a practice getting reviews consistently ranks higher than one with all old reviews
  • Responding to reviews is a ranking signal — Google rewards engagement
  • Keywords in reviews help you rank for those terms — patients who write "Botox" help you rank for Botox

Section 02
The Review Ask
Timing, exact words, and the system that gets reviews consistently without feeling like begging
Most providers either never ask for reviews or ask in a way that feels awkward and gets ignored. The review ask is a skill — and like every skill, it gets easier and more effective with practice and the right system behind it.
From Danielle
"The single biggest thing that built my review count was simply asking. Out loud. In person. While the patient was still in the chair. Not a text link two days later — in the moment, when they're looking in the mirror and they love what they see. That moment is gold. Don't waste it."
The 3 Rules of the Review Ask
★
Ask in the moment — not after. The best time to ask is when the patient is still in your chair, looking in the mirror, and happy. That emotional high is when they're most likely to follow through. A text link 48 hours later hits them when they're distracted and the feeling is gone.
★
Make it personal, not transactional. "It would really help our small practice" lands differently than "Please leave us a Google review." You're not a corporation asking for a rating. You're a person asking for a favor.
★
Remove every obstacle. The easier you make it, the more reviews you get. Text them the direct link. Walk them through opening Google if they're in the office. Make the path from "yes" to "done" as short as possible.
The In-Person Ask — Word for Word

While patient is looking in the mirror — post-treatment

In Person
What to say
"I'm so glad you love it — that genuinely makes my day. Can I ask you a favor? If you have 60 seconds before you leave, a Google review means the world to a small practice like ours. It's literally how people find us. I'll text you the link right now so you don't have to search for it — would that be okay?"
Why this works: You asked for a favor, not a rating. You gave them a time frame (60 seconds — feels manageable). You removed the obstacle by offering to text the link. You explained why it matters to a small business. And you asked for permission before sending — which makes the text feel welcomed, not spammy.

The follow-up text — send within 5 minutes of them leaving

Text Message
Text script
"Hi [Name]! It was so great seeing you today 🤍 Here's that Google review link I mentioned — takes about 60 seconds and means everything to us: [YOUR GOOGLE REVIEW LINK]. Thank you so much! — [Your name], [Practice Name]"
Send this while they're still in the parking lot. Response rate drops dramatically after 30 minutes. The link should go directly to your Google review page — not your homepage. Get your direct review link at: business.google.com → your profile → Get more reviews → Share review form.

The email follow-up — 24 hours later if no review yet

Email
Email script
"Hi [Name] — just wanted to check in and make sure you're loving your results! If you have a moment, we'd be so grateful for a Google review — it's the #1 way new patients find us and it takes less than a minute. Here's the link: [LINK]. Thank you for trusting us with your care — we can't wait to see you next time. 🤍"
One follow-up only. Sending more than one follow-up after the initial ask feels pushy and can damage the relationship. If they haven't reviewed after the text and one email — let it go.
Building the Review System — So It Runs Automatically

Create a saved text on your phone with the review link

Go to your phone's keyboard shortcuts. Create a shortcut — type "rev" → expands to your full review text with the Google link. You can send it in 3 taps from any appointment.

Set up an automated post-appointment text in your booking system

Fresha and most booking platforms allow automated follow-up messages. Set one to fire 2–4 hours post-appointment with your review link. This runs on autopilot for every single booking.

Create a QR code for your front desk

Generate a QR code from your Google review link (free at qr-code-generator.com). Print it on a small card at checkout. "Scan here to leave us a review" — takes 10 seconds.

Add your review link to every email signature

"Love your results? ⭐ Leave us a Google review: [link]" at the bottom of every email you send. Passive review generation at zero effort.

Track your review count weekly

Every Monday, check your Google Business Profile. How many new reviews this week? What's the trend? Treat it like a business metric — because it is one.

Section 03
The 24-Hour Bad Review Protocol
Exactly what to do in the first 24 hours after a negative review drops — step by step

The most important rule: Do NOT respond to a bad review while you are emotional. Screenshot it, close the tab, give yourself at least one hour before you touch it. Responses written in anger, defensiveness, or panic always make things worse. Always.

The Protocol — Hour by Hour
1
0–5 minutes

Screenshot and document everything

Screenshot the review with the date, star rating, reviewer name, and full text visible. Save it. If this escalates legally later, you need the original documentation. Do not respond yet.

2
0–60 minutes

Identify the reviewer

Do you recognize the name? Can you match it to a patient record? Was this person actually a patient? If you cannot identify them at all — this may be a fake review (see Section 7). If you can identify them — pull their chart and review their visit notes before you respond to anything.

3
1–6 hours

Attempt private outreach before public response

If you can identify the patient, reach out privately first — by phone or text. A private conversation has a much higher chance of resolution than a public one. Many bad reviews get updated or removed once the patient feels heard. Use the scripts in Section 5.

4
Within 24 hours

Post your public response

Whether or not the private outreach worked, respond publicly within 24 hours. A review with no response looks worse than a review with a professional response. Use the scripts in Section 4. Keep it brief, warm, and never defensive. See Section 6 for HIPAA rules before you write a single word.

5
24–72 hours

Request a review from your next 5 happy patients

The best response to a bad review is burying it with good ones. The moment a negative review drops, activate your review-getting system immediately. Five new 5-star reviews push the negative one down the page and dilute its impact on your overall rating.

6
If applicable

Flag for removal if it violates Google's policies

Not every bad review can be removed — but some can. See Section 7 for the exact conditions under which Google will remove a review and how to report it. Do not assume all bad reviews are permanent.

Section 04
Word-for-Word Response Scripts
Every type of negative review — and exactly what to say publicly
From Danielle
"Your public response isn't for the person who left the review. They've already decided how they feel. Your response is for every potential patient who reads it afterward. That shift in thinking changed everything about how I write responses."

Read Section 6 before writing any response

HIPAA prohibits confirming or denying that someone is a patient, disclosing any treatment details, or sharing any health information in a public response — even to correct a false claim. Violating HIPAA in a Google response has resulted in federal investigations. Read Section 6 first.

The Unhappy With Results Review

Most Common
Patient leaves 1–2 stars saying they're unhappy with their results, don't see a difference, or feel it wasn't worth the money.
Public response
"Thank you for sharing your experience — I'm genuinely sorry to hear you're feeling this way. Your satisfaction matters deeply to us and we'd love the opportunity to make this right. Please reach out to us directly at [phone/email] so we can connect with you personally. We take every piece of feedback seriously and want to ensure you feel taken care of."
What you never say: specific treatment details, that you did everything correctly, that results vary. What you always say: acknowledgment, invitation to connect offline, genuine care.

The False or Inaccurate Review

High Stakes
Review contains information that is factually incorrect — wrong date, wrong service, claims something happened that didn't, or describes a situation that doesn't match your records.
Public response
"Thank you for your feedback. We take every review seriously and want to make sure every experience at our practice is a positive one. We weren't able to match this review to a visit in our records — if you'd be willing to reach out to us directly at [phone/email], we'd genuinely appreciate the chance to connect and understand your experience better. We're always here."
You are not confirming or denying they were a patient. You are not calling them a liar publicly. You are inviting them to connect — which signals to readers that you are professional and open. Never say "we have no record of you" — that's a HIPAA issue.

The Rude / Aggressive Review

Emotionally Hardest
Review uses aggressive, threatening, or personally attacking language toward you or your staff.
Public response
"We're sorry to hear about your experience and we understand you're frustrated. We always strive to provide compassionate, professional care to everyone who walks through our doors and we take this feedback seriously. We'd welcome the opportunity to speak with you directly — please reach out at [phone/email] if you'd like to connect."
Do not match their energy. Do not defend yourself against specific accusations publicly. The more aggressive their review and the calmer your response — the better you look to every person reading. Readers can see who the professional is.

The Pricing Complaint

Common
Review complains that your prices are too high, that they felt overcharged, or that they can get the same service cheaper elsewhere.
Public response
"Thank you for your feedback. We understand that price is an important factor and we always want our patients to feel that the value they receive reflects the quality of care we provide. We'd love to chat with you more about your experience — please feel free to reach out to us at [phone/email]. We appreciate you taking the time to share your thoughts."
Never justify your prices in a public response. Never compare yourself to cheaper options. The patients reading your response who pay your prices don't want to see you apologize for them. Stay confident and warm.

The Wait Time / Scheduling Complaint

Operational
Review complains about waiting too long, scheduling difficulty, or feeling rushed during their appointment.
Public response
"Thank you so much for this feedback — this is exactly the kind of thing we need to hear. Your time is valuable and we never want anyone to feel like their appointment wasn't given the attention it deserved. We're actively working to improve and your feedback directly helps us do that. Please reach out at [phone/email] — we'd love to make this right."
Operational complaints are actually the easiest to respond to because you can genuinely commit to improvement. Readers respect humility and accountability far more than defensiveness.

The Partially Valid Complaint

Hardest to Write
Review describes something that was partially your fault — a miscommunication, a wait, a staff interaction that could have gone better.
Public response
"Thank you for being honest with us — this feedback matters. We hold ourselves to a high standard and when we fall short of that, we want to know. We're sorry your experience wasn't what it should have been and we'd genuinely love the chance to make it right. Please reach out at [phone/email] — we're listening."
This is the response that builds the most trust with potential patients. Accountability without over-apologizing. You're not admitting legal liability — you're showing that you're the kind of practice that takes feedback seriously and does something about it.
Section 05
Turning a 1-Star Into a Loyal Patient
The private outreach scripts that resolve complaints before they become permanent
A patient who complains loudly is a patient who cares enough to say something. In 10 years of practice, some of Danielle's most loyal long-term patients started as unhappy ones. The difference was a phone call.
From Danielle
"I've called patients after bad reviews more times than I can count. Most of the time they're shocked that I personally called. Not a staff member. Me. And that one call — where I listened and didn't defend — turned the situation around more often than you'd believe. Two of those patients are still coming to see me today."
The Private Outreach — Phone Call Script

The personal phone call — within 6 hours of the review

Highest Impact
What to say when they pick up
"Hi [Name], this is [Your name] calling from [Practice Name]. I saw your review and I wanted to call you personally — not have a staff member reach out, me. I'm genuinely sorry that your experience wasn't what it should have been. Can you tell me a little more about what happened? I really want to understand."
Then — just listen. Don't defend. Don't explain. Don't justify. After they've finished:
"Thank you for telling me that. That's not the experience I want anyone to have here. Here's what I'd like to do — [offer: complimentary follow-up / partial credit / whatever is appropriate]. Would you be open to coming back so I can personally make sure you leave happy? That's genuinely what I want."
The call itself — regardless of outcome — often results in a review update or removal. Even if it doesn't, you've done the right thing. Document the call in their chart.

The text outreach — if phone call goes to voicemail

Text
Text script
"Hi [Name] — this is [Your name] from [Practice Name]. I saw your review and wanted to reach out personally. I'm sorry your experience wasn't what it should have been. Would you be open to a quick call? I'd really like to hear more and see how I can make this right. You can reach me directly at this number. — [Name]"
Personal. Direct. Not a template. The fact that it's you texting — not an automated system — matters enormously. Keep it short. Don't over-explain in a text.
How to Ask for a Review Update — Without Demanding It

After resolution — asking them to update their review

Optional — Only If Resolution Was Genuine
What to say
"I'm so glad we got to connect and that we were able to make this right. If you ever feel moved to update your Google review to reflect your full experience — including how we handled things — I would be incredibly grateful. But there's absolutely no pressure. What mattered most to me was that you left feeling taken care of."
Never demand a review update as a condition of resolution. Never offer something of value in exchange for a review update — that violates Google's policies. Ask once, genuinely, and drop it. An updated review that goes from 1 star to 4 or 5 is one of the most powerful things on your profile — it shows future patients that you fix problems.
Section 06
The HIPAA Line
What you can never say in a public review response — and what has gotten providers investigated

This is not optional reading. Providers have faced federal HIPAA investigations for disclosing patient information in Google review responses. The Office for Civil Rights takes these complaints seriously. One wrong sentence in a public response can cost far more than the review ever would have.

What HIPAA Prohibits in Public Review Responses

Confirming the person was a patient

"We remember your visit on..." or "When you came in for your Botox..." — even if what you say is positive, confirming someone is a patient discloses PHI.

Mentioning any treatment details

Any reference to what service they received, what product was used, what their skin concern was — all PHI. Even "the treatment you received" is problematic if it confirms a medical relationship.

Contradicting their medical claims publicly

A patient says "the Botox didn't work." You cannot say "actually your results were excellent based on your follow-up photos." You cannot reference their chart, their photos, or any clinical finding.

Disclosing why they were declined treatment

"We were unable to treat you because..." — if the reason relates to any health condition, medication, or contraindication, it is PHI.

Sharing information to defend yourself against a false claim

Even if a patient is lying publicly about what happened — you cannot use their medical information to prove they're wrong. Your protection is your documentation and your attorney — not a public response.

The Safe Framework for Every Public Response

Every public response must follow this pattern

  • Acknowledge the feeling — never the facts of the visit
  • Express genuine care — without confirming a treatment relationship
  • Invite them offline — phone number or email, move it out of public
  • Never reference — specific dates, services, products, clinical outcomes, or chart information
  • Never deny — that they were a patient or that something happened
  • Keep it short — 3–5 sentences maximum. Every additional sentence is another opportunity to make a mistake.

When to call your attorney instead of responding

If a review contains defamatory statements (false statements of fact, not just negative opinions), threatens legal action, or describes a serious clinical allegation — contact your malpractice carrier and a healthcare attorney before you respond to anything publicly. Some situations require legal guidance before any public statement is made.

Section 07
Fake & Competitor Reviews
How to identify them, report them to Google, and fight back the right way
From Danielle
"I've had fake reviews. Reviews from people who were never my patients. Reviews that described treatments I don't even offer. It's infuriating — and the process for getting them removed is slow and imperfect. But it works if you know how to do it correctly. This section is that process."
How to Identify a Fake Review

No match in your records

Strong indicator

You cannot identify the reviewer's name, profile photo, or any detail that matches a patient record. Cannot be matched to any appointment in your system.

Describes services you don't offer

Very strong indicator

Review mentions a treatment, product, or experience that isn't part of your service menu. This is almost always a fake or misdirected review.

Reviewer profile is new or empty

Moderate indicator

Click the reviewer's profile. One review total, created recently, no photo, no other activity. Not definitive — but combined with other indicators, suspicious.

Similar language to competitor reviews

Moderate indicator

The phrasing, specific complaints, or language pattern matches negative reviews on competitor profiles. Coordinated review attacks exist in the med spa industry.

Wrong location or wrong business

Strong indicator

Review describes a location, parking situation, or physical detail that doesn't match your practice. May be intended for a different business entirely.

Posted during or after a known conflict

Contextual indicator

Review appears immediately after a staff termination, a known dispute with a patient, or following a public conflict. Timing matters as evidence.

How to Report a Fake Review to Google — Step by Step
1

Document everything before reporting

Screenshot the review, the reviewer's profile, the date it was posted, and any other reviews on their profile. Save all of this. If Google removes the review, you lose the ability to screenshot it afterward.

2

Flag the review through Google Business Profile

Google Business Profile → Reviews → find the review → click the three dots → "Report review." Select the most accurate reason: Spam, Off topic, Conflict of interest, or Not a real customer experience. Submit.

3

Submit a formal removal request

Go to: business.google.com/support → contact support → explain that the review is fraudulent and does not represent a real customer experience. Provide your documentation. Be specific and factual — not emotional.

4

Follow up if no response in 7 days

Google's initial review of reports is automated and often results in "doesn't violate our policies." If this happens, request a human review by responding to their decision email or re-contacting support with additional evidence.

5

If Google won't remove it — your response becomes your defense

Some fake reviews never get removed. In that case, your public response does the work: "We take every review seriously — however, we were unable to match this review to any visit in our records. If you've had a genuine experience with us, please contact us directly at [phone/email] so we can connect." This tells every reader exactly what they need to know without accusing anyone publicly.

Competitor-planted reviews — what you can do

If you have strong evidence that a competitor is planting fake reviews, document everything and consult an attorney about defamation claims. In egregious cases, legal action is appropriate — especially if the reviews contain false statements of fact. Keep records of any coordinated pattern across multiple fake reviews.

Section 08
The Review Moat
Volume as your defense — why 200 reviews at 4.9 is untouchable
A review moat is what happens when your volume of positive reviews is so high that a single bad review — real or fake — cannot meaningfully impact your rating. This is the long game. And it is the most powerful reputation protection strategy that exists.
The math that changes everything
"When Hello Gorgeous had 30 reviews, a 1-star review moved my rating noticeably. When we had 200 reviews, a 1-star review moved it by 0.02. The moat is real. Volume is everything. One bad review in a sea of good ones is a speedbump. One bad review when you only have 15 is a crisis."
What Your Moat Needs to Be Bulletproof

Volume targets by practice size

  • Under 1 year: Goal is 50 reviews — every patient asked every time
  • Year 1–2: Goal is 100 reviews — automated system running
  • Year 3–5: Goal is 200+ reviews — moat is established
  • Year 5+: Maintain 10+ new reviews per month — recency matters as much as volume
  • The danger zone: Any practice with fewer than 50 reviews is vulnerable to a single bad review having major rating impact

Recency — why old reviews don't protect you

  • Google weights recent reviews more heavily than old ones
  • A practice with 200 reviews but none in 6 months looks stagnant
  • Patients notice when the most recent review is from 8 months ago
  • Target: at least 4–6 new reviews per month minimum for an active practice
  • A review sprint after a bad review is not cheating — it is managing your reputation actively
The Review Sprint — 30 Patients in 30 Days

Pull your patient list from the last 6 months

Identify every patient you've seen who had a good experience and has NOT left a review. This is your sprint list.

Send a personal text to 5 patients per day for 6 days

Not a mass email — individual texts. Personalize each one with their name and a reference to their last visit. "Hi [Name] — I was thinking about your [treatment] results and wanted to check in. If you have a second, a Google review would mean the world to us: [link]"

Ask every in-person patient during the sprint

Every single patient in the chair during your sprint gets the in-person ask. No exceptions for 30 days.

Add a review CTA to your Instagram stories for one week

"If you've been a patient with us and love your results — we'd be so grateful for a Google review. Link in bio. 🤍" Simple, genuine, effective.

Track daily and celebrate milestones

Post your team's running review count somewhere visible. Celebrate every 10 new reviews. Make it a practice-wide goal — everyone who interacts with patients is part of the review culture.
