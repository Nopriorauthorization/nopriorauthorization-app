Hormone Therapy — Overview & Scope of Practice

What BHRT is, who can prescribe it, and why it belongs in your aesthetic practice

Read this first — scope of practice is everything

Hormone therapy prescribing scope varies significantly by state and by license type. NPs and PAs can prescribe in most states independently or with a collaborative agreement. MDs and DOs prescribe independently. Estheticians and non-prescribers cannot prescribe hormones — ever. Before building a hormone program, verify your state's prescribing authority and collaborative agreement requirements. This playbook is an operational guide — not a substitute for legal and regulatory review of your specific license and state.

What BHRT is — and why patients are seeking it

Bioidentical Hormone Replacement Therapy (BHRT)

BHRT uses hormones that are chemically identical to those naturally produced by the human body — estradiol, progesterone, testosterone, DHEA, and others. Unlike synthetic hormones, bioidentical hormones are derived from plant sources and compounded or manufactured to match the body's natural molecular structure. Patients seek BHRT for: perimenopause and menopause symptoms, fatigue, weight gain, low libido, brain fog, mood instability, sleep disruption, and loss of muscle mass.

Why this belongs in your aesthetic practice

The natural connection to aesthetics

  • Hormonal decline directly causes skin thinning, collagen loss, hair thinning, and weight redistribution — the same concerns aesthetic patients present with
  • A patient on optimized hormones responds better to aesthetic treatments — better skin quality, better healing, better filler retention
  • Hormone patients are high-retention — monthly visits, quarterly labs, ongoing management
  • Average hormone patient LTV: $2,400–$4,800/year before any aesthetic services
  • Natural cross-sell: hormone patients are already invested in their wellness and open to aesthetic services

How to incorporate — the three models
1

In-house prescribing (NP/PA/MD)

You prescribe, manage, and monitor. Highest revenue. Highest responsibility. Requires prescribing authority, collaborative agreement if required, lab ordering capability, and a compounding pharmacy relationship. Full clinical accountability stays with your practice.

2

Collaborative model — partner with a hormone specialist

You identify and refer, the specialist prescribes, you administer (if within scope) and co-manage the aesthetic component. Revenue share or referral arrangement. Lower liability, lower revenue. Good starting point.

3

Telehealth platform integration

Partner with a telehealth hormone platform (Biote, Evexias, Winona, Midi Health). They handle prescribing. You administer pellets or injections if applicable and earn a facility fee. Simplest entry point — least infrastructure required.

Patient Screening & Consultation

Who is a candidate, who isn't, and how to open the conversation

Opening consultation script
"Tell me how you've been feeling — not just physically, but overall. Energy, mood, sleep, your relationship with your body. I want the full picture before we talk about any options."
Hormone consultations are emotional. Women have often been dismissed by other providers for years before arriving at your door. Lead with listening. The clinical assessment comes after they feel heard.
Ideal hormone therapy candidates

Symptoms that indicate hormone evaluation

  • Perimenopause or menopause — hot flashes, night sweats, irregular cycles, vaginal dryness
  • Fatigue that doesn't resolve with sleep — especially mid-afternoon crashes
  • Low libido or sexual dysfunction — in both women and men
  • Brain fog, difficulty concentrating, memory concerns
  • Mood instability — anxiety, irritability, depression not fully explained by other causes
  • Unexplained weight gain — especially abdominal, despite diet and exercise
  • Loss of muscle mass or difficulty building muscle despite training
  • Hair thinning or loss beyond normal shedding
  • Sleep disruption — difficulty falling or staying asleep
Contraindications — absolute and relative

Absolute contraindications — do not prescribe estrogen

  • Personal history of estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer
  • Undiagnosed abnormal vaginal bleeding
  • Active or recent DVT, PE, or arterial thromboembolic disease
  • Active liver disease or liver failure
  • Known or suspected pregnancy
  • Endometrial cancer (estrogen without progestogen)

Relative contraindications — discuss risk/benefit carefully and document

History of migraines with aura, hypertriglyceridemia, active gallbladder disease, uterine fibroids (size-dependent), history of clotting disorders, strong family history of hormone-sensitive cancers. These require individualized risk-benefit discussion, shared decision-making, and thorough documentation.

Baseline labs — required before prescribing

Female hormone panel — minimum baseline

  • Estradiol (E2) — baseline level, guides dosing
  • Progesterone — especially important in perimenopausal patients
  • Total and free testosterone — often deficient in women; impacts libido, energy, muscle
  • FSH and LH — confirm menopausal status
  • DHEA-S — adrenal hormone, often declines with age
  • SHBG (sex hormone binding globulin) — affects free hormone availability
  • TSH, free T3, free T4 — thyroid function; overlapping symptoms with hormone imbalance
  • Complete metabolic panel — liver function, kidney function, glucose
  • CBC — baseline blood counts
  • Lipid panel — cardiovascular baseline
  • Fasting insulin — metabolic health indicator
  • Vitamin D, B12 — commonly deficient; affect similar symptoms

Male hormone panel — testosterone optimization

  • Total testosterone — baseline; low T defined as <300 ng/dL by most guidelines
  • Free testosterone — more clinically relevant than total in symptomatic patients
  • Estradiol (sensitive assay) — men convert testosterone to estrogen; monitor to prevent excess
  • LH and FSH — differentiate primary vs. secondary hypogonadism
  • PSA — baseline and monitoring for men on testosterone therapy
  • Hematocrit/CBC — testosterone increases RBC production; monitor for polycythemia
  • CMP, lipids, thyroid — full metabolic baseline

Hormones, Delivery Methods & Dosing Protocols

The major hormones, how they're delivered, and general dosing frameworks

Important — dosing is individualized

Hormone dosing is not a one-size-fits-all protocol. Every dose should be based on the patient's labs, symptoms, body weight, age, and clinical response. The ranges below are general starting frameworks — not fixed protocols. Always dose to symptom resolution and optimal lab values, not to a number on a chart.

Clinical rule — always prescribe progestogen with systemic estrogen if uterus is present

DHEA — the adrenal androgen precursor

Clinical overview

  • Available OTC as a supplement — also compounded for specific dosing
  • Typical dose: 25–50mg daily oral; 10–25mg for women (lower doses)
  • Prasterone (Intrarosa) — FDA-approved vaginal DHEA for dyspareunia in menopause
  • Baseline DHEA-S required before supplementing — check adrenal status
  • Caution: can convert to androgens — monitor for androgenic side effects in women
Follow-up and monitoring schedule
1

6–8 weeks post-initiation

Repeat hormone panel. Assess symptom response. Adjust dosing based on labs AND symptoms — not labs alone. Some patients need levels at high-normal range for symptom resolution.

2

3 months

Full repeat panel. Assess tolerability. Check for androgenic side effects if testosterone prescribed. PSA in men. Hematocrit for testosterone patients.

3

Every 6 months (stable patients)

Maintenance labs. Reassess need for continued therapy. Annual mammogram reminder for estrogen patients. Continued PSA monitoring for men.

Vendors, Compounding Pharmacies & Lab Partners

Who to work with, what to look for, and how to vet your partners

Vendor vetting is a patient safety responsibility

Compounded hormones are not FDA-approved finished drug products. Quality, potency, and sterility vary between compounding pharmacies. You are responsible for the quality of what you prescribe. Always use PCAB-accredited compounding pharmacies and verify 503A or 503B status depending on your practice model.

Lab partners — for in-house or send-out testing

LabCorp / Quest Diagnostics

Reference Lab

Standard reference labs. Widely accepted by insurance. Broad hormone panel availability. Most patients already familiar. Order through your EMR or paper requisition. Results in 24–72 hours typically.

→ Default choice for most practices — familiar to patients, insurance-friendly

ZRT Laboratory

Specialty Lab  ·  Saliva/Blood Spot

Specialty hormone testing including saliva, dried blood spot, and urine. Particularly useful for monitoring topical hormone absorption (saliva reflects tissue levels better than serum for transdermal). Good for functional medicine approach.

→ Use when serum testing doesn't correlate with symptoms

Dutch Test (Precision Analytical)

Specialty  ·  Urine Hormone Metabolites

Dried urine testing for comprehensive hormone panel including metabolites. Shows how the body is processing and breaking down hormones — information serum tests miss. More expensive but clinically valuable for complex cases.

→ Best for complex or refractory hormone cases — not needed for routine monitoring

Midi Health / Winona / Alloy

Telehealth Prescribing

Telehealth platforms prescribing menopause and hormone therapy. Option to refer patients who need prescribing but your scope or model doesn't support it. Some allow affiliate/referral arrangements. Patients receive pharmacy-direct medications.

Do's & Don'ts — Hormone Therapy in Practice

The operational rules that protect your patients and your license

Do — always

  • Obtain and review baseline labs before prescribing any hormone
  • Document the clinical indication for every prescription
  • Use shared decision-making — document that alternatives were discussed
  • Schedule follow-up labs at 6–8 weeks after any initiation or dose change
  • Prescribe progestogen with systemic estrogen in patients with a uterus
  • Monitor PSA in all men on testosterone therapy
  • Monitor hematocrit in all testosterone patients — men and women
  • Use PCAB-accredited compounding pharmacies only
  • Document patient symptoms at every visit — not just labs
  • Maintain a collaborative agreement if required by your state
  • Recommend annual mammograms for women on estrogen therapy
  • Keep consent forms current and reviewed by a healthcare attorney

Don't — never

  • Prescribe hormones without a clinical indication and documented labs
  • Dose to a number — dose to symptom resolution within safe lab ranges
  • Use synthetic progestins (medroxyprogesterone) when bioidentical progesterone is appropriate and available
  • Prescribe without a follow-up plan — abandonment is a liability
  • Use unaccredited compounding pharmacies or unverified online pharmacies
  • Prescribe estrogen without addressing progestogen need
  • Market hormone therapy with unsubstantiated cure or anti-aging claims
  • Prescribe outside your scope of practice or state prescribing authority
  • Ignore patient symptoms even if labs are "within range"
  • Miss PSA monitoring in men — malpractice exposure is significant
  • Prescribe to pregnant patients or those trying to conceive without specialist co-management
Incorporating into your aesthetic practice — the smart workflow
1

Add hormone symptom screening to your aesthetic intake form

Ask every patient about energy, sleep, libido, mood, and weight — even if they came in for Botox. This surfaces hormone candidates you'd otherwise miss. "Have you noticed changes in your energy, weight, or mood in the last year?" opens the door.

2

Offer a hormone consultation as a standalone appointment

45–60 minutes. $150–$250 consultation fee (may apply toward first month of care). Covers symptom history, lab orders, and treatment plan discussion. Separate from aesthetic appointments — this is a medical visit.

3

Build the monthly management visit

$150–$200/month. Labs every 3–6 months at additional cost. This is your recurring revenue engine. A hormone patient who stays with you is worth $1,800–$2,400/year before any aesthetic services.

4

Cross-sell naturally — not aggressively

"Your skin quality will actually improve as your hormones optimize — estrogen directly supports collagen production. A lot of my hormone patients see a noticeable difference in their skin texture over the first 3–6 months." That's education, not sales.

Patient FAQs — Hormone Therapy

The questions every hormone patient asks — answered clearly and compliantly

What is the difference between BHRT and traditional HRT?▾
Traditional HRT often uses synthetic hormones that are similar but not chemically identical to the body's natural hormones. Bioidentical hormone replacement therapy (BHRT) uses hormones that are chemically identical to what your body naturally produces — estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone in their natural molecular form. Many patients and providers prefer BHRT because of this molecular similarity, though it's important to understand that "natural" and "safe" are not the same thing — all hormone therapy carries risks that we discuss thoroughly before starting.
Does hormone therapy cause breast cancer?▾
This is the question I get most often, and it deserves a thorough answer. The evidence is nuanced. The 2002 WHI study raised concerns about combined estrogen-progestin HRT and breast cancer risk — however, this study used synthetic progestins, not bioidentical progesterone, and oral conjugated equine estrogen rather than bioidentical estradiol. More recent data, including the E3N cohort study, suggests that bioidentical progesterone combined with estradiol does not carry the same risk as synthetic progestins. The risk profile is not zero, but it is meaningfully different from what the original headlines suggested. We discuss this in full at your consultation and weigh it against the known risks of untreated menopause.
How long does it take to feel a difference?▾
Most patients notice improvement in sleep and hot flashes within 2–4 weeks. Energy and mood improvements typically follow at 4–8 weeks. Libido and physical changes — muscle, body composition, skin quality — take longer, often 3–6 months of consistent therapy. Hormone optimization is not a quick fix. It's a process of finding the right dose and delivery method for your body, and that takes time. We monitor closely in the first 3 months to get your levels and symptoms dialed in.
Do I have to take hormones forever?▾
Not necessarily — but for many patients, hormone therapy is a long-term choice. Menopause is permanent. The symptoms and health implications of hormone deficiency — bone loss, cardiovascular risk, cognitive changes, vaginal atrophy — don't resolve on their own. Many patients choose to continue therapy indefinitely because the quality-of-life benefits outweigh the risks for them personally. We reassess annually and the decision to continue is always yours. If you choose to stop, we discuss how to taper safely.
Can women take testosterone?▾
Yes — and this is one of the most underutilized treatments in women's health. Women naturally produce testosterone in the ovaries and adrenal glands, and levels decline significantly with age and at menopause. Low testosterone in women contributes to fatigue, low libido, difficulty building muscle, brain fog, and mood changes. We prescribe testosterone for women at much lower doses than for men — the goal is restoring physiologic levels, not creating male hormone levels. It is prescribed off-label in the US, which we discuss fully in your consent.
What will my labs cost?▾
Initial hormone panels typically run $200–$500 depending on the breadth of testing, whether you use insurance, and the lab we use. We'll discuss your options at your consultation. Some panels are covered by insurance if ordered for specific diagnoses — we can help you navigate this. Follow-up monitoring labs are typically less extensive and less expensive than the initial panel.
Is compounded hormone therapy safe?▾
Compounded hormones are prepared by licensed, accredited pharmacies under rigorous quality standards. We use only PCAB-accredited compounding pharmacies, which meet the highest quality standards in the industry. The main difference from FDA-approved products is that compounded medications go through a different quality oversight process. For many patients — especially those needing customized doses or delivery methods not available in commercial products — compounded hormones are the most appropriate choice. We discuss this at your consultation.
Can hormone therapy help my skin?▾
Yes — and this is a genuine, clinically supported benefit, not just a marketing claim. Estrogen directly stimulates collagen production, maintains skin thickness and hydration, and supports wound healing. As estrogen declines in perimenopause and menopause, skin thins, loses collagen, and becomes drier — often by 30% in the first 5 years after menopause. Restoring estrogen can meaningfully slow this process. Testosterone also supports collagen and elastin. Patients on optimized hormone therapy often notice improved skin texture and firmness within 3–6 months. It's one of my favorite benefits to see.
