Intradermal (ID)

10–15° · 26–30g · 1–2mm
Technique
Position: Bevel UP — always
Angle: 10–15° nearly parallel to skin
Insertion: Advance just under skin surface — 2–3mm
Endpoint: Visible wheal (blister-like bump) confirms correct placement
No aspiration — intradermal layer has no vessels of concern
No massage after — disrupts wheal
Med Spa Applications
Skinvive/HA microdroplets — cheek intradermal grid
Botox — periorbital lower lid (advanced)
Local anesthetic wheal before cannula entry
Allergy skin testing (not typically in med spa)
Volume per deposit: 0.01–0.05mL maximum
Common error: Too steep angle = SubQ — no wheal forms

Subcutaneous (SubQ)

45–90° · 25–31g · 4–10mm
Technique
Pinch technique: Grasp subcutaneous fat between thumb and finger — lifts away from muscle
Angle: 45° for lean patients; 90° for patients with more subcutaneous fat
Insertion: Quick, smooth — insert to hub or appropriate depth
Aspiration: Not required for SubQ (vessels rare) — but standard for testosterone
Inject slowly: Slow pressure to minimize discomfort and site reaction
Release pinch before withdrawing to prevent tracking
Med Spa Applications
GLP-1 (semaglutide, tirzepatide) — weekly injection
Peptide injections (BPC-157, CJC-1295, TB-500, etc.)
Testosterone cypionate — women (low dose)
Vitamin/mineral injections (alternative to IM)
Best sites: Abdomen (2" from navel), thigh, upper arm
Rotate sites: Lipohypertrophy risk with repeated same-site injection

Intramuscular (IM)

90° · 21–25g · 25–38mm
Technique
Angle: 90° — straight into muscle
Spread skin: Z-track technique or spread skin taut (not pinch)
Aspirate: Pull back plunger 5–10 seconds before injecting — blood = wrong placement, reposition
Inject rate: 1mL per 10 seconds. Do not inject faster.
Withdraw smoothly at same angle as insertion
Apply pressure 30 seconds post-injection. Light massage (unless Botox/specific product)
Med Spa Applications
B12, B-complex — deltoid or ventrogluteal
MIC/Lipo-C injections — deltoid preferred
Glutathione IM — deltoid
Testosterone cypionate — men (ventrogluteal preferred)
Vitamin D (high dose) — deltoid or ventrogluteal
Biotin — deltoid

Intravenous (IV)

15–30° · 18–22g · Into vein lumen
Peripheral IV Technique
Apply tourniquet 3–4 inches above site — dilates vein
Palpate vein — feel for bounce and track. Do not insert blind.
Angle: 15–30°, bevel up, pierce skin then advance at shallower angle into vein
Flashback: Blood return in catheter chamber confirms vein entry
Advance catheter off needle, withdraw needle, release tourniquet
Flush with 10mL NS before connecting drip — confirm patency
Med Spa IV Applications
IV hydration (NS, LR) — rapid onset
NAD+ infusion — 1–4 hours depending on dose
Myers cocktail (Mg, B-complex, Vit C, B12)
High-dose vitamin C — immune support
Glutathione IV push — brightening, antioxidant
Must assess: BP and pulse before every IV infusion

Z-Track Technique — When & Why
What it isPull skin 2–3cm laterally before injection. Release after withdrawing. Creates a zigzag path that seals medication in muscle and prevents tracking to surface.
When to useIron dextran (mandatory), any medication that stains skin or is highly irritating, oil-based hormones (testosterone), any IM injection where leakage is a concern
Technique1) Clean site. 2) Pull skin lateral with non-dominant hand. 3) Insert needle at 90°. 4) Aspirate. 5) Inject slowly. 6) Wait 10 seconds. 7) Withdraw needle. 8) Release skin. 9) Apply pressure — do NOT massage (disturbs seal).
Never massage after Z-trackMassaging breaks the Z-track seal — allows medication to track back to SubQ layer or skin surface
Injection Complications — Recognition & Response
Blood in aspirateWithdraw needle. Do not inject. Apply pressure. Prepare new dose in new syringe. Select alternate site.
HematomaApply firm pressure 2–5 minutes. Elevate if limb. Document. Monitor for expansion. Ice after 24 hours.
Abscess / infectionWarmth, redness, swelling days after injection. Antibiotics. Incision and drainage if fluctuant. Review sterile technique.
LipohypertrophyFatty lump from repeated same-site SubQ injection. Prevention: site rotation every injection. No treatment once present except site avoidance.
Nerve strike (IM)Patient reports sharp shooting pain or numbness during IM injection. Withdraw immediately. Do not inject. Reassess landmark. Most resolve fully.
AnaphylaxisUrticaria + hypotension + bronchospasm = anaphylaxis. Epinephrine 0.3–0.5mg IM (thigh) IMMEDIATELY. Call 911. Lay patient supine, legs elevated. Have protocol posted and epi auto-injector in every room.
Aspiration — When Required, When Optional, When Not Needed
RouteAspirationRationale
IntradermalNot requiredDermal layer has no large vessels. Wheal formation confirms correct placement.
SubcutaneousNot required (current CDC/WHO guidance)SubQ layer has minimal vascular risk for standard medications. Exception: some providers still aspirate for testosterone SubQ.
Intramuscular — deltoid, thighNot required per current CDC guidance for vaccinesLow vessel density. However — many med spa providers aspirate for all IM injections as a safety standard. Either approach is defensible.
Intramuscular — ventrogluteal/dorsoglutealAspirate — standard practiceCloser proximity to superior gluteal artery. Aspirate 5–10 seconds before injecting. Blood = reposition.
IntravenousFlashback confirms placementBlood return in catheter confirms vein entry. Flush with NS to confirm patency before connecting drip.
★ One needle, one patient, one time. Never recap a used needle. Never reuse a syringe. Never use a single-use vial for multiple patients.
★ Sharps in sharps containers immediately after withdrawal. Do not set on counter, pass to another person, or carry across the room.
★ Room temperature medications inject more comfortably — oil-based testosterone especially. Remove from refrigerator 30 minutes prior.
★ Slow injection = less pain. 1mL per 10 seconds for IM. Pressure pain is caused by rapid bolus delivery — not the needle. Train every provider on injection speed.
★ Epinephrine must be in every injection room. Every room. Every day. Every patient. Anaphylaxis is rare — but when it happens, seconds matter.
